Most adults now identified with ADHD were identified *as adults* — in a 2023 nationally representative US survey, *"approximately one half received the diagnosis at age ≥18 years"* (Staley et al., 2024), against criteria that require symptoms before age 12. That tension is the subject of this note. It matters here because the case this notebook tracks is exactly that shape: an adult, no childhood diagnosis, one positive screen.

## The finding that unsettled the field

Three prospective birth cohorts, published within a year of each other, independently found that adult-identified and childhood-identified ADHD populations barely overlap.

The Dunedin cohort reported that *"the childhood-ADHD and adult-ADHD groups comprised virtually non-overlapping sets; 90% of adult-ADHD cases lacked a history of childhood ADHD,"* and — critically — that the adult group *"did not show tested neuropsychological deficits in childhood or adulthood, nor did they show polygenic risk for childhood ADHD"* (Moffitt et al., 2015). The UK E-Risk twin cohort found *"[a]mong 166 individuals with adult ADHD, 112 (67.5%) did not meet criteria for ADHD at any assessment in childhood"* (Agnew-Blais et al., 2016). The Brazilian Pelotas cohort found only 17.2% of childhood cases persisted and only 12.6% of young-adult cases had childhood ADHD, concluding the data *"suggest the existence of 2 syndromes that have distinct developmental trajectories"* (Caye et al., 2016).

Taken at face value, that would mean adult ADHD is often not a continuation of anything.

## Why "late-onset" is probably the wrong word

It was not taken at face value. When Sibley et al. (2018) re-examined the MTA comparison group using eight repeated multi-informant assessments and a stepped diagnostic procedure, *"approximately 95% of case subjects who initially screened positive for late-onset ADHD were excluded from diagnosis."* Most exclusions happened because *"symptoms or impairment were better explained by heavy substance use or another mental disorder"* — the same confounding-by-mimics problem that runs through [[dimensions/measurement|the measurement note]], reappearing at cohort scale.

The cases that did survive scrutiny looked like masking, not onset. Sibley describes individuals with *"above-average childhood ADHD symptoms and superior intellect"* and concludes: *"Since symptoms were likely present but mitigated in childhood, these individuals might better be characterized as late-identified, rather than late-onset, ADHD cases."* This is `hypothesis:late-identified-not-late-onset`, and it is the reading this notebook adopts — while keeping the cohort findings on the record as the live rival (`tension:late-onset-vs-late-identification`).

The proposed mechanism is not a change in the trait but a change in the load against it. Sibley calls it *"developmental misfit"*: *"Mounting environmental demands in adolescence may temporarily exacerbate above-average but subthreshold childhood ADHD symptoms … or create cognitive overload."* Impairment, on this view, is a person–environment fit problem — which is why capable people can cross a diagnostic threshold in their thirties without anything new having happened to their neurology.

## The course fluctuates

The persist-or-remit dichotomy also turns out to be false. Across 16 years of MTA follow-up, *"[o]nly 9.1% of the children with ADHD demonstrated recovery … and only 10.8% demonstrated stable ADHD persistence across all time points. For most of the sample (63.8%), the follow-up period was characterized by fluctuating persistence and remission"* (Sibley et al., 2022). The authors draw the consequence directly: *"If ADHD tends to wax and wane, factors modulating phenotypic expression must be identified and person-environment fit emphasized as a crucial framework for evaluation and treatment over time"* (`hypothesis:adhd-course-fluctuates`).

This is the temporal counterpart of the dimensional argument in [[literature/dimensional-continuum-adhd|the continuum survey]]. Not only is there no natural cut across people at a moment; there is no stable cut within one person across time.

## Thresholds, prevalence, and the onset criterion

DSM-5 lowered the adult symptom threshold to five and raised the age-of-onset criterion from 7 to 12. A field trial in 18–19-year-olds supported the first — *"[t]he best cut-offs were five symptoms of inattention and four symptoms of hyperactivity/impulsivity"* — while estimating *"a 27% increase in the expected prevalence of ADHD among young adults, comparing DSM-IV to DSM-5 criteria"* (Matte et al., 2015). A global meta-analysis shows how much the definition drives the number: requiring childhood onset gives 2.58% prevalence, scoring current symptoms alone gives 6.76% (Song et al., 2021). Both decline steadily with age.

The honest summary is that "how many adults have ADHD" is largely a question about where the threshold sits and whether retrospective childhood recall is required — not a fact waiting to be measured. That is what a continuum with an arbitrary cut looks like in epidemiology.

## What it means for this case

Three things follow for the personal record. First, the absence of a childhood diagnosis is weak evidence against ADHD — a majority of adult diagnoses are made without one, and masking by cognitive strength is a documented pathway. Second, retrospective childhood recall is unreliable enough that a clinician should probe it rather than accept a yes/no. Third, and least comfortable: the *same* cohort literature shows that most adult presentations screening positive are better explained by something else, which is the `tension:adhd-vs-anxiety-internal-restlessness` differential arriving from a new direction. Late identification is a real and common path; it is also the path with the highest false-positive rate. Both are true, and only an evaluation separates them.
