This note is about a measurement artifact with consequences: the ADHD sex ratio changes depending on *where you count*. That gap is one of the cleanest demonstrations in the field that an instrument plus a referral pathway can manufacture an apparent fact about a disorder.

## The ratio depends on the sampling frame

Reported male:female ratios *"ranged from 2:1 to 10:1 … with higher male-to-female ratios found in clinical vs. population-based samples"* (Slobodin & Davidovitch, 2019). Community ascertainment lands near the bottom of that range: a large US community sample found *"a male:female ratio of 2.28:1,"* and the authors concluded that *"[l]ower male:female ratios than reported in some clinic-based studies suggest that females are underdiagnosed in the community"* (Ramtekkar et al., 2010).

The sharpest version comes from a single Swedish cohort measured both ways. Among register-diagnosed children the ratio was ~2.5:1; among children meeting DSM-5 symptom criteria it was ~1.8:1. Within the high-symptom group, *"303 (18.5%) of the males with elevated symptoms, and 111 (12.1%) of the females with elevated symptoms had an ADHD diagnosis recorded"* (Mowlem et al., 2019). Same population, same year — the diagnostic filter, not the trait, produced the difference (`hypothesis:sex-ratio-reflects-ascertainment`).

By adulthood the gap largely closes: *"even in representative samples, the sex ratio of diagnosed ADHD approaches 1.5:1 or lower during the adult years"* (Hinshaw et al., 2022). A childhood ratio that converges in adulthood is what under-detection looks like, not what a sex-linked prevalence difference looks like.

## The filter is externalizing behaviour

The mechanism is visible in what predicts referral. Willcutt's meta-analysis notes that *"the predominantly inattentive type is the most common subtype in the population, but individuals with the combined type are more likely to be referred for clinical services"* (Willcutt, 2012) — a structural filter that removes quiet presentations regardless of sex. Layered on top, hyperactive and conduct symptoms predicted diagnosis and medication more strongly in girls, which Mowlem et al. read as evidence that *"externalising behaviours drive referral for ADHD"* and that *"[f]emales with ADHD may be more easily missed in the ADHD diagnostic process and less likely to be prescribed medication unless they have prominent externalising problems."*

Presentation differences compound it: *"[g]irls and women with ADHD show a predominance of inattention and associated internalizing problems; boys and men display greater levels of hyperactive–impulsive symptoms and associated externalizing problems"* — with the crucial rider that these differences *"depend heavily on the clinical versus nonreferred nature of the samples"* (Hinshaw et al., 2022). Much of what is described as a sex difference in ADHD is partly a sex difference in *who reaches a clinic*.

Compensation closes the loop, connecting this note to [[dimensions/adult-late-diagnosis|late identification]]: *"many girls with ADHD, particularly those with predominant inattention, may compensate with intensive effort and coping strategies (including family coping), masking key impairments and lowering the chance for recognition"* (Hinshaw et al., 2022).

## The criteria and instruments are male-referenced

This is where it becomes a measurement problem rather than only a clinical one. *"[S]ymptom lists for ADHD are biased toward male behaviors (e.g., physical overactivity or extreme risk-taking) as opposed to female-related manifestations"* (Hinshaw et al., 2022), and Mowlem et al. conclude that *"the current diagnostic criteria and/or clinical practice are somewhat biased towards the male presentation of ADHD."* The instruments inherit their samples: in the DSM-5 and ICD-11 ADHD field trials, *"only 21% of participants were girls"* (Platania et al., 2025).

The consequence for the framework in [[dimensions/orthogonality|the orthogonality map]] is specific. A rating scale does not measure a trait directly; it measures behaviour *as described by items chosen from a mostly-male reference sample*, filtered by an observer whose referral threshold is itself sex-biased. Two of the four levels — phenotype and its instrumentation — carry that bias, while the mechanism and etiology levels are less exposed to it. It is another reason a single rating score is a weak anchor (`hypothesis:no-single-measure-is-diagnostic`).

## What it means here

The direct relevance to this case is limited — the record is an adult man, the demographic *least* filtered out by referral bias. But two things carry over. The general lesson is that base rates and ratios in this literature are properties of measurement systems as much as of people, which is a reason to hold any prevalence figure loosely. And the specific lesson is that the predominantly *inattentive* presentation is under-referred in everyone, not only in women — the quiet presentation is structurally invisible to a system triggered by disruption, which is one more route to arriving at a first screening in adulthood.
