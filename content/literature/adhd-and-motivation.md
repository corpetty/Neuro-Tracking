"Why can I do the things I care about but not the things I should?" is the motivation puzzle of ADHD, and it is the source of a lot of self-blame. The research reframes it: motivation in ADHD is not a matter of willpower or character but of how the brain's reward system drives behaviour. This note lays out that reframe and its limits. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-motivation.jsonl`.

## 1. Motivation is a third axis, with a neural signature

ADHD is usually described in two dimensions, but a third keeps surfacing. Volkow et al. (2011) put it directly: *"Attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is typically characterized as a disorder of inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity but there is increasing evidence of deficits in motivation"* (`mechanism:reward-motivation`). And they gave it a neural basis: *"Using positron emission tomography (PET), we showed decreased function in the brain dopamine reward pathway in adults with ADHD, which, we hypothesized, could underlie the motivation deficits in this disorder"* (`hypothesis:adhd-involves-motivation-reward-dysfunction`). Lower trait motivation tracked reduced dopamine reward-pathway function in the ADHD group but not in controls.

## 2. The behavioural side: sensitivity to reinforcement

The same difference shows up in how reward shapes behaviour. Luman et al.'s (2005) review frames it as *"an aberrant sensitivity to reinforcement, such as reward and response cost"* as a candidate core deficit. Crucially, this is *modifiable by incentives*: *"reinforcement contingencies have a positive impact on task performance and levels of motivation for both children with AD/HD and normal controls. There is evidence that the effect related to task performance is somewhat more prominent in AD/HD"* (`hypothesis:adhd-performance-is-contingency-dependent`). And the reward has to be soon — *"[c]hildren with AD/HD prefer immediate over delayed reward"* — which is the timing side of the story, the delay aversion tracked in `mechanism:reward-delay-aversion`.

## 3. The reframe: contingency-dependent, not incapable

Put together, these say the ADHD performance gap is not a fixed inability but a *reward-dependence*. Performance rises with interest, urgency, novelty and reward, and falls without them — which is exactly why the same person can lose a weekend to an absorbing project ([[literature/adhd-and-hyperfocus|hyperfocus]]) and be unable to start a dull ten-minute form. Morsink et al. (2017) add nuance from the inside: *"[i]ndividuals with ADHD appear to respond differently to incentives than their peers. This could be due to a general altered sensitivity to reinforcers."* The felt "I can only do things I find interesting" has a reward-processing basis; it is not laziness.

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **It reframes the self-blame.** "I'm just unmotivated / lazy / undisciplined" is the moral reading of what §1–3 describe as a reward-processing difference. Contingency-dependent output — brilliant under deadline, stalled without one — is a signature of the pattern, not a character verdict.
- **It is one pathway, not the diagnosis.** Reward/motivation is one of several dissociable routes into ADHD (`hypothesis:multiple-neurocognitive-pathways`, developed in [[literature/adhd-and-executive-function|executive function]]), present in some more than others and not unique to ADHD, so it describes rather than proves.
- **Useful to name, carefully.** If work only happens under interest or urgency, that is a concrete, reportable pattern for an [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]] — and a reason to build reward and structure into how tasks get done, regardless of how the diagnostic question resolves.
