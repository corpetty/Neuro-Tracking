"Time blindness" — losing track of how long things take, being surprised by deadlines, feeling time slip away — is one of the more concrete complaints in adult ADHD, and unlike some popular labels it has a solid research base in the study of timing and temporal processing. This note covers what is well established, what the felt experience corresponds to, and where the evidence stops short. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-time-perception.jsonl`.

## 1. The deficit is real and broad

Timing problems in ADHD are among the better-replicated cognitive findings. Noreika et al.'s (2013) integrative review of neurocognitive and neuroimaging work concluded that *"ADHD patients are consistently impaired in three major timing domains, in motor timing, perceptual timing and temporal foresight, comprising several timeframes spanning milliseconds, seconds, minutes and longer intervals up to years"* (`hypothesis:adhd-impairs-time-perception`). Toplak et al. (2006) name the specific abilities affected: *"growing evidence links ADHD to problems in several aspects of temporal information processing, including duration discrimination, duration reproduction, and finger tapping"* (`mechanism:temporal-processing`). This is not a soft or purely subjective phenomenon; it shows up on controlled tasks and implicates cerebellar, basal-ganglia and prefrontal circuitry.

## 2. What "time blindness" corresponds to

The lived complaint maps onto those measured deficits. Ptacek et al. (2019), reviewing the clinical side, report that *"individuals with ADHD have difficulties in time estimation and discrimination activities as well as having the feeling that time is passing by without them being able to complete tasks accurately and well."* That sentence links the two halves — the objective estimation/discrimination impairment and the everyday sense of time getting away from you. It also fits the wider reframe in this notebook: time sense is bound up with attention regulation and executive control, so impaired timing sits alongside the [[literature/adhd-and-executive-function|executive-function]] and attention-dysregulation accounts (`hypothesis:adhd-is-attention-dysregulation-not-deficit`) rather than standing apart from them.

## 3. Real, but not a diagnostic test

Because the deficit is consistent, some go further. Ptacek et al. (2019) propose impaired time perception as *"a possible diagnostic characteristic"* of ADHD (`hypothesis:time-perception-candidate-diagnostic-marker`). That is where caution is due (`tension:time-perception-marker-vs-nonspecific`). Timing deficits are not specific to ADHD — they appear across other neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions — the paradigms used vary widely, and even the supportive reviews hedge: Toplak et al. open their finding with *"[w]hile further replication is needed."* A robust group-level difference is not the same as a marker that classifies an individual, which is the same limit that applies to imaging and executive-function tests elsewhere here (`hypothesis:no-single-measure-is-diagnostic`).

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **The experience is worth reporting, precisely.** Concrete instances — chronically misjudging how long a task will take, deadlines that arrive "suddenly", losing hours without noticing — are legitimate, well-grounded observations to bring to an [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]], not vague self-criticism.
- **It describes, it does not diagnose.** Time-perception trouble is real and impairing but non-specific (§3), so its presence supports the attention-regulation picture without confirming ADHD over the mimics ([[literature/adhd-and-anxiety|anxiety]], [[literature/adhd-and-mood|mood]], [[literature/adhd-and-sleep|sleep]]), which can disrupt timing too.
- **No task will settle it.** As with the executive-function tests, do not expect a timing task to prove or exclude the diagnosis; it is one more strand of the regulation story, weighed clinically.
