Impulsivity is half of ADHD's name and one of its two symptom domains, but "impulsive" turns out to be a bundle of different things wearing one word. This note unpacks the bundle — the distinct facets, their different evidence, and the awkward fact that none of them is unique to ADHD. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-impulsivity.jsonl`.

## 1. Impulsivity is not one thing

The foundational correction is that impulsivity fractionates. Winstanley et al. (2006) state it plainly: *"it has been suggested that impulsivity is not a unitary construct, but encompasses a variety of related phenomena that may differ in their biological basis"* (`hypothesis:impulsivity-is-multidimensional`), measured by distinct paradigms and mapping to distinct circuits. Nigg (2017) situates it in the wider self-regulation system, arguing that *"the field needs a domain-general construct of SR that encompasses SR of action, emotion, and cognition"* — which is what makes room for an *emotional* facet alongside the cognitive and motor ones.

The facets that matter here:

- **Motor / response inhibition** — failing to stop a prepotent action, indexed by the stop-signal task ([[literature/adhd-and-executive-function|executive function]], `mechanism:response-inhibition`).
- **Choice / delay** — preferring a smaller-sooner reward over a larger-later one. Jackson & MacKillop (2016) found *"robust evidence that delay discounting is significantly elevated among individuals with ADHD compared to controls"* (`mechanism:reward-delay-aversion`) — the [[literature/adhd-and-motivation|reward/delay]] facet.
- **Emotional urgency** — acting rashly under strong emotion, which ties impulsivity to the [[literature/adhd-and-anxiety|emotional-dysregulation]] axis (`mechanism:emotional-dysregulation`).

These dissociate: someone can discount delayed rewards steeply while inhibiting actions normally, or vice versa, which is exactly the dissociable-pathways picture (`hypothesis:multiple-neurocognitive-pathways`). "Impulsive" without specifying which facet says little.

## 2. And it is not ADHD-specific

The second correction matters for the differential. Impulsivity is shared. Winstanley et al. note it is *"a component of numerous psychiatric illnesses including attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), mania and substance abuse"* (`hypothesis:impulsivity-is-transdiagnostic-not-adhd-specific`), and the inhibition facet in particular is not specific: Lipszyc & Schachar (2010) found *"medium deficits in stop signal reaction time (SSRT), reflecting the speed of the inhibitory process, for attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) (g = 0.62), obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) (g = 0.77) and schizophrenia (SCZ) (g = 0.69)"* — comparable across three very different disorders. So impulsivity is a real, measurable feature of ADHD that nonetheless characterises without discriminating.

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **Say which impulsivity.** An impulsivity item on the ASRS is a starting point, not an endpoint: §1 says the useful question is *which* facet — snapping out actions, chasing immediate over delayed rewards, or acting on strong feeling — because they mean different things and point at different mechanisms.
- **It describes, it does not diagnose.** §2 says impulsivity is transdiagnostic, so its presence supports the ADHD picture without confirming it over the mimics ([[literature/adhd-comorbidity-overview|and comorbidities]]) — the same "real but shared" caveat that recurs throughout this notebook.
- **The emotional facet links the threads.** If the impulsivity is more emotional urgency than motor haste, that connects to the internal-state and emotional-dysregulation material rather than the classic "acts without thinking" stereotype — worth distinguishing when describing it at an [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]].
