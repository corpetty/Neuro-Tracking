The costs of ADHD are not only internal. They land on the people closest — partners who feel unheard or over-relied-on, arguments that escalate faster than they should, forgotten commitments read as not caring. This note sets out what the research shows about ADHD and relationships, and, usefully, *what* about ADHD does the damage. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-relationships.jsonl`.

## 1. Real strain, by several measures

The association is consistent across relationship types. In couples, Eakin et al. (2004) found that *"married adults with ADHD reported poorer overall marital adjustment on the Dyadic Adjustment Scale (DAS; Spanier, 1989) and more family dysfunction on the Family Assessment Device (FAD; Eptein, Baldwin, & Bishop, 1983) than control adults."* In younger romantic relationships, Bruner et al. (2015) found that *"[p]articipants who reported clinically significant levels of both hyperactivity-impulsivity and inattentiveness (consistent with ADHD-C) had lower relationship quality than those whose self-reported symptoms indicated no ADHD diagnosis"* (`hypothesis:adhd-impairs-relationship-quality`). And at the family level, Wymbs et al. (2008) found that *"parents of youths diagnosed with ADHD in childhood (n = 282) were more likely to divorce and had a shorter latency to divorce compared with parents of children without ADHD (n = 206)"* — the strain runs through raising a child with ADHD as well as being an adult with it.

## 2. What carries the damage: emotion, not just inattention

The more useful question is *which* part of ADHD strains relationships, and the answer is not simply "forgetting things." Bruner et al. (2015) tested the pathway and found that *"[e]motion regulation problems and hostile relationship conflict mediated this association"* (`hypothesis:emotion-dysregulation-mediates-adhd-relationship-impact`). So the relationship impact ties back to the emotional-dysregulation axis developed in [[literature/adhd-and-anxiety|ADHD & anxiety]] (`mechanism:emotional-dysregulation`) — quick escalation, difficulty repairing after conflict — as much as to inattention or impulsivity. The everyday version is familiar: the problem is often less the missed task than the flare that follows being reminded of it.

## 3. Not one-sided, and not fixed

Two nuances keep this honest. First, perception differs across the couple: Eakin et al. noted that adults with ADHD viewed their marriages more negatively than their spouses did, and that spouses often quietly *compensated* for their partners' difficulties — so the picture is not simply "the ADHD partner is the problem." Second, it is addressable: Eakin et al. concluded that the findings underscore the need for assessments and treatments that address marital and family functioning in adults with ADHD. Naming the pattern, and treating the emotional-regulation piece, changes the dynamic.

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **Relationship friction may be a symptom's footprint.** If conflicts escalate around forgotten or half-done things, or a partner has drifted into a managing role, §1–2 suggest that is a recognised consequence of the pattern, driven substantially by emotional regulation — worth noting at an [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]] rather than filed as "just how I am."
- **It connects to the other costs.** Relationship strain feeds and is fed by [[literature/adhd-and-self-esteem|self-esteem]] and mood, so it belongs in the [[literature/adhd-comorbidity-overview|whole picture]] rather than treated in isolation.
- **It is a target, not a fate.** §3 says assessment and treatment (including the emotional-regulation piece) can shift the dynamic, and that both people's perspectives count — a more workable framing than blame in either direction.
