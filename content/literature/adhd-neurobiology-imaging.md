This note adds the brain. It has two halves, and the second is the important one: what the neurobiology *is* (distributed, network-level), and what it emphatically *is not* (a scan that can diagnose you). Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-neurobiology-imaging.jsonl`.

## 1. A mechanism: default-mode interference

The most influential mechanistic idea connects the "wandering mind" directly to a brain network. Sonuga-Barke & Castellanos (2007) proposed that *"spontaneous patterns of very low frequency (<0.1 Hz) coherence within a specific brain network ('default-mode network') thought to support a pattern of generalized task-non-specific cognition during rest, can persist or intrude into periods of active task-specific processing, producing periodic fluctuations in attention that compete with goal-directed activity."* The observable consequence is the inconsistency that shows up on reaction-time tasks: they *"explore the putative role of default-mode interference as a cause of performance variability in attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder."*

This gives `mechanism:default-mode-interference` a concrete substrate and ties it to the `mechanism:reaction-time-variability` already tracked here, and to the mind-wandering axis in [[dimensions/arousal-and-mind-wandering|arousal & mind-wandering]]. It is a model of *why* attention lapses, not a diagnostic sign.

## 2. The biology is distributed, not focal

The older picture of ADHD as a prefrontal-striatal problem has widened. Castellanos & Proal (2012) argue that *"[r]ecent advances in systems neuroscience-based approaches to brain dysfunction have facilitated the development of models of ADHD pathophysiology that encompass a number of different large-scale resting-state networks."* There is no ADHD "spot" in the brain; the account is network-level and distributed across frontoparietal, dorsal-attention, motor, visual and default systems (`hypothesis:adhd-neurobiology-is-distributed-not-focal`). A distributed, graded substrate fits the dimensional phenotype better than a discrete lesion would.

## 3. The findings are real but tiny — and in adults, absent

This is the half that matters for a person deciding what a brain scan could tell them. The largest structural studies exist, they are well-powered, and they find *real* group differences — which are also *very small*.

The ENIGMA subcortical mega-analysis (Hoogman et al., 2017; 1,713 with ADHD, 1,529 controls) reported that *"[t]he volumes of the accumbens (Cohen's d=-0·15), amygdala (d=-0·19), caudate (d=-0·11), hippocampus (d=-0·11), putamen (d=-0·14), and intracranial volume (d=-0·10) were smaller in individuals with ADHD compared with controls in the mega-analysis."* Effect sizes near d = −0.1 to −0.2 are small by any convention — a large overlap between groups, nowhere near separable at the individual level. The companion cortical study (Hoogman et al., 2019) found the same order of magnitude: *"the largest significant effect was for total surface area (Cohen's d=-0.21)."*

And then the finding that speaks most directly to this case: *"[n]either surface area nor thickness differences were found in the adolescent or adult groups."* In adults, the cortical differences were not merely small — they were undetectable. So structural imaging cannot function as a diagnostic test (`hypothesis:brain-differences-small-not-diagnostic`), least of all for an adult. This is the imaging-specific version of the rule already established from the guidelines, where the World Federation consensus concluded that ADHD cannot be diagnosed by rating scales, neuropsychological tests, or brain imaging ([[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|the adult assessment]], `hypothesis:no-single-measure-is-diagnostic`).

## Where this leaves the personal case

The neurobiology is a good antidote to two opposite errors. It refutes the idea that ADHD is "not real" — there are replicable group-level brain differences and a coherent network model. And it refutes the idea that a scan could settle anything for one person — the effects are small, distributed, and in adults absent. Neither a normal-looking brain nor an unusual one would change the diagnostic picture, which rests on the clinical and structural evidence in [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|the assessment note]], not on imaging. The etiology level of the dimensions map is now genetics plus a distributed, small-effect neurobiology — both consistent with the continuum, neither diagnostic on its own.
