Emotional dysregulation has come up again and again in this notebook: as what ADHD shares with [[literature/adhd-and-anxiety|anxiety]] and [[literature/adhd-and-mood|mood]], as the root of [[literature/adhd-and-rejection-sensitivity|rejection sensitivity]], as the "urgency" facet of [[literature/adhd-and-impulsivity|impulsivity]], as what carries much of the strain in [[literature/adhd-and-relationships|relationships]], and as the most persistent feature of one [[literature/adhd-biotypes|brain-derived biotype]]. This note gives it a page of its own. It covers how big the effect is, whether it belongs to ADHD itself or to something co-occurring, what it costs, how well treatment reaches it, and where it blurs into other conditions. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source (PubMed abstracts, or PMC full text for Pan et al.) and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-emotional-dysregulation.jsonl`.

"Emotional dysregulation" here means difficulty modulating emotional responses: quick temper, low frustration tolerance, rapid mood shifts (emotional *lability*), and a slow return to baseline (`mechanism:emotional-dysregulation`). The literature also uses narrower labels, such as *emotional impulsivity* and *deficient emotional self-regulation*, and the proliferation of terms is part of the problem (§2).

## 1. It is large, and it scales with ADHD

The effect is not subtle. A meta-analysis of 13 studies (N = 2,535) comparing adults with ADHD to healthy controls found that *"adults with ADHD revealed significantly higher levels of general ED (Hedges' g = 1.17, p < 0.001; Hedges' g is the adjusted effect size)"* (Beheshti et al., 2020). Emotional lability was the strongest facet (g = 1.20), and general ED correlated with ADHD symptom severity at r = 0.54. For comparison, the structural brain differences in [[literature/adhd-neurobiology-imaging|neurobiology & imaging]] sit around d = 0.1–0.2. Emotional dysregulation is one of the largest group differences in adult ADHD.

It also doesn't seem to be comorbidity in disguise. Skirrow & Asherson (2013) screened over 500 adult referrals to find ADHD cases *without* comorbidity, medication or frequent substance use, and concluded: *"EL in adults with ADHD appears to be primarily associated with ADHD itself rather than comorbid conditions, and helps to explain some of the impairments not accounted for by classical features of the disorder."* In their data, lability was best predicted by hyperactivity-impulsivity, not by subsyndromal anxiety or mood symptoms. That is directly relevant to the rival-explanations rule in this notebook. It's a result against "it's all just anxiety", though from a small, all-male sample (41 cases).

## 2. Core symptom, or correlated neighbour? Still unsettled

The authors of both papers above read their results as support for "core". Beheshti et al.: *"Our findings support ED symptoms as a core feature of ADHD's psychopathology."* Faraone et al.'s (2019) practitioner review is more careful. It grants that *"emotional impulsivity (EI), and deficient emotional self-regulation (DESR) may be sufficiently specific for ADHD to function as diagnostic criteria"*, and then adds the catch: *"there is no consensus about how to define these constructs sin a manner that would be specific to the disorder"* (sic, "sin" is in the source).

This is the same split the notebook already records from Shaw et al. (2014), whose three candidate models run from "correlated but distinct dimensions" through "core diagnostic feature" to "a nosological entity distinct from both". So `hypothesis:emotional-dysregulation-is-core-adhd` stays *being-tested*. The case for "core" has got stronger (large, severity-linked, not explained by comorbidity), but the specificity problem is unsolved. Emotional symptoms are common across psychiatric conditions, and there is no agreed way to measure the ADHD-specific kind. DSM-5 does not list it as a criterion.

## 3. It carries impairment of its own

Whatever its status, it matters functionally. Faraone et al.: *"Emotional symptoms in ADHD cause clinically significant impairments."* Skirrow & Asherson found lability *"contributed independently to impairment in daily life"*, over and above the classic inattentive and hyperactive symptoms (`hypothesis:emotional-dysregulation-independently-drives-impairment`). This fits the [[literature/adhd-and-relationships|relationships]] finding that emotion regulation and conflict carry much of ADHD's relational cost. It also fits the [[literature/adhd-biotypes|biotype]] result, where in children *"biotype 1 showed more persistent symptoms compared to the marked decreases observed in biotypes 2 and 3"* on deficient emotional self-regulation. Emotional dysregulation is often the component that lingers.

## 4. Medication helps, but about half as much

A meta-analysis of nine double-blind RCTs in adults (Moukhtarian et al., 2017) found that *"stimulants and atomoxetine were moderately effective for EL symptoms, while effect size on core ADHD symptoms was twice as large"* (SMD −0.41 vs −0.8). Their suggested explanation is *"EL may be less responsive than ADHD symptoms overall, perhaps indicating the need for adjunctive psychotherapy in some cases"* (`hypothesis:emotional-dysregulation-less-medication-responsive`). The implication: if emotional dysregulation is a prominent part of the picture, medication alone may leave it partly untouched. Skills-based work (emotion-regulation training, DBT-informed approaches; see [[literature/adhd-and-coping-strategies|coping strategies]] and [[literature/adhd-interventions|interventions]]) is the natural complement. And, as the interventions note warns, how the emotional side responds to treatment is not evidence for or against the diagnosis.

## 5. Where it blurs: bipolar and borderline

Emotional dysregulation is the feature that makes adult ADHD hardest to separate from bipolar disorder (BD) and borderline personality disorder (BPD). Asherson et al. (2014) give the practical differentiators (`hypothesis:time-course-separates-adhd-from-bipolar-and-bpd`):

- **Time course against bipolar disorder.** *"BD is episodic, with periods of normal mood although not necessarily function. In patients with comorbid ADHD-BD, ADHD symptoms are apparent between BD episodes."* ADHD emotional reactivity is trait-like and moment-to-moment. Bipolar mood states are episodes lasting days to weeks, with a baseline in between.
- **Specific features against BPD.** BPD and ADHD are both chronic, and *"Overlapping symptoms of BPD and ADHD include impulsivity and emotional dysregulation. Symptoms of BPD but not ADHD include frantically avoiding real/imagined abandonment, suicidal behavior, self-harm, chronic feelings of emptiness, and stress-related paranoia/severe dissociation."*
- They also co-occur: *"ADHD coexists in ∼20% of adults with BD or BPD"*. So, as the [[literature/adhd-comorbidity-overview|comorbidity overview]] keeps finding, the answer can be "and".

The anxiety and depression side of this differential is covered in [[literature/adhd-and-anxiety|ADHD & anxiety]] and [[literature/adhd-and-mood|ADHD & mood]]. The general rule from those notes applies here too: *intensity* doesn't discriminate, *structure* does (onset, time course, triggers, what's present between flare-ups).

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **Not yet on the record.** The case file so far is an inattentive-leaning ASRS screen plus a disruptive internal state. It doesn't describe emotional reactivity as such, and nothing here should be read as saying it's present. What this note contributes is a question to answer honestly, not a finding.
- **If it is part of the picture, name it separately.** Given §1 and §3, emotional lability (quick irritation, frustration spikes, slow cool-down) is a real, measurable, impairing component. It's worth describing on its own at an [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]], because standard ADHD criteria and the ASRS don't ask about it directly.
- **It sharpens the internal-state question.** The "churning inside" could be emotional reactivity, anxious arousal, or low mood. Skirrow & Asherson suggest ADHD lability can stand on its own without comorbidity. The differential cue is the one from the anxiety and mood notes: reactive and fast-cycling (ADHD-like) versus worry-driven (anxiety) versus persistent low baseline (mood) versus discrete episodes (bipolar).
- **Treatment expectations.** If it's prominent and ADHD is confirmed, §4 suggests planning for skills-based support alongside any medication, rather than expecting medication alone to settle the emotional side.
