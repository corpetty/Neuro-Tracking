Mood is the third mimic named in the personal differential, alongside anxiety and sleep (`mechanism:mood-disturbance` — "depression / chronic stress"). This note completes that triad. As with [[literature/adhd-and-sleep|sleep]], the relationship is not a one-way look-alike: depression can mimic ADHD, but ADHD also *raises the risk of* depression, so the two can be genuinely entangled. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-mood.jsonl`.

## 1. Depression and ADHD co-occur

Low mood is among the conditions that most often accompany ADHD — Katzman et al. (2017) note that *"[a]s many as 80% of adults with ADHD have at least one coexisting psychiatric disorder,"* with mood and anxiety disorders the most frequent. Prospectively, the risk is elevated: Meinzer et al. (2013) found that *"[a]dolescents with a lifetime history of ADHD were at significantly higher risk of MDD through early adulthood relative to those with no history of ADHD"* (`hypothesis:adhd-mood-frequently-comorbid`). So depression and ADHD have to be considered together, not as either/or.

## 2. ADHD raises the risk of depression — plausibly causally

This is the strand that distinguishes mood from a simple mimic. The ADHD→depression link survives serious scrutiny. Meinzer et al. (2013) report that *"ADHD remained a significant predictor of MDD after controlling for gender, lifetime history of other psychiatric disorders in adolescence, social and academic impairment in adolescence, stress and coping in adolescence, and new onset of other psychiatric disorders through early adulthood (hazard ratio, 1.81; 95% confidence interval, 1.04, 3.06),"* so the effect is not just confounding by comorbidity or hardship.

Riglin et al. (2021) went further with Mendelian randomization, which uses genetic liability to probe causality: *"Our findings suggest that ADHD increases the risk of depression later in life and are consistent with a causal effect of ADHD genetic liability on subsequent major depression"* (`hypothesis:adhd-raises-depression-risk`). The intuitive pathway is demoralisation — years of underperformance, missed deadlines and strained relationships wear mood down — but shared genetics contribute too.

## 3. Three readings, and a practical discriminator

That leaves the same open question as the other differentials (`tension:adhd-mood-directionality`). Poor concentration, low drive, and psychomotor and sleep changes belong to *both* depression and ADHD, so given someone with low mood and inattention, the mood could be:

1. **A mimic** — the inattention is depression-driven, not ADHD (`mechanism:mood-disturbance`).
2. **A consequence** — ADHD came first and bred the depression (§2).
3. **Shared liability** — common genetic and environmental factors produce both.

Unlike a mimic that simply competes with the ADHD reading, the consequence route means both can be true at once — which is why this is held open rather than decided. The most useful discriminator is **course**. Depression is typically *episodic*: it descends, lasts weeks to months, and lifts, and the concentration problems track the episode. ADHD is *chronic and trait-like*: present across settings and years, including long stretches of stable or good mood. The same logic separates ADHD from **bipolar disorder**, where the discriminator is again episodicity — sustained mood episodes with a change from baseline, versus ADHD's constant, reactive, fast-shifting emotional lability (`mechanism:emotional-dysregulation`, developed in [[literature/adhd-and-anxiety|ADHD & anxiety]]). That is a differential a clinician should hold, though nothing in the personal record currently raises bipolar specifically.

## Where this leaves the personal case

The practical questions are about *time course*, not intensity:

- **Is the inattention episodic or lifelong?** Attention problems confined to low periods point to depression; ones present across years and moods point toward ADHD.
- **Does the internal state lift when mood is good?** If the "churning inside" eases in stable periods, mood is carrying more of it; if it persists regardless, less so.
- **Both can hold.** If ADHD is present, §2 says depression may be partly downstream of it, so finding low mood does not rule ADHD out — it may be a reason the picture is worse. This links to the foggy, internalising pole of the internal state ([[literature/internal-restlessness-and-cognitive-disengagement|cognitive disengagement]], which tracks depression) and, as ever, is settled by the structural evidence a full [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]] gathers, not by any single low week.
