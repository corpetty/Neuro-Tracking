"Executive function" is the phrase most often reached for to explain ADHD, and much of this notebook's machinery — response inhibition, working memory, the trade-off task in the [[literature/ADHD-traits-inhibition-learning|Horváth study]] — sits inside it. This note pulls the executive-function (EF) thread together: the theory that puts EF at the centre of ADHD, the evidence that complicates it, and the awkward gap between what an EF test measures and what executive trouble feels like in a life. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-executive-function.jsonl`.

## 1. The theory: ADHD as an inhibition deficit

The most influential account makes one process primary. Barkley (1997) proposed that *"[a]ttention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) comprises a deficit in behavioral inhibition. A theoretical model is constructed that links inhibition to 4 executive neuropsychological functions that appear to depend on it for their effective execution: (a) working memory, (b) self-regulation of affect-motivation-arousal, (c) internalization of speech, and (d) reconstitution (behavioral analysis and synthesis)."* On this view (`hypothesis:adhd-as-executive-inhibition-deficit`), inhibition is the root and the other executive weaknesses are branches — an organising idea that also explains why emotional dysregulation and restlessness travel with attention problems (`mechanism:executive-function`).

## 2. The evidence: real, but neither necessary nor sufficient

The empirical picture is more textured than a single core deficit. Willcutt et al.'s (2005) meta-analysis of 83 studies confirmed the deficits are real and broad: *"Groups with ADHD exhibited significant impairment on all EF tasks. Effect sizes for all measures fell in the medium range (.46-.69), but the strongest and most consistent effects were obtained on measures of response inhibition, vigilance, working memory, and planning."* But the same review delivered the decisive caveat: *"moderate effect sizes and lack of universality of EF deficits among individuals with ADHD suggest that EF weaknesses are neither necessary nor sufficient to cause all cases of ADHD"* (`hypothesis:ef-deficits-neither-necessary-nor-sufficient`).

That is the crux (`tension:ef-core-vs-one-component`). Barkley's model treats EF as the core of ADHD; Willcutt's data show EF deficits in only a subgroup, not specific to ADHD, and of moderate size. A substantial number of people with ADHD perform normally on EF tests. So EF is *one important component of a heterogeneous neuropsychology*, which is exactly the dissociable-pathways picture already in this notebook (`hypothesis:multiple-neurocognitive-pathways`): inhibition, delay aversion and timing deficits occur separately, and no one of them is the disorder.

## 3. The test–life gap: a normal EF test proves little

There is a further trap, and it matters clinically. What an EF *test* measures in a quiet clinic room and what executive dysfunction *does* to a life diverge sharply. Toplak et al. (2013) found that across studies, questionnaire ratings and performance tests of the "same" executive construct barely agree: *"[o]nly 68 (24%) of the 286 relevant correlations reported in these studies were statistically significant, and the overall median correlation was only .19."* And when the outcome is real-world functioning, the ratings win: Barkley & Fischer (2011), following hyperactive children to adulthood, *"conclude that EF ratings are better predictors of impairment in major life activities generally and occupational functioning specifically at adult follow-up than are EF tests"* (`hypothesis:rating-task-dissociation`).

The implication is blunt: a person can pass the lab EF tests and still be genuinely impaired by executive problems in daily life. A clean continuous performance test or working-memory task does not rule ADHD out.

## Where this leaves the personal case

Three consequences follow, and they line up with the rest of the notebook.

- **An EF test is not a verdict, in either direction.** Because EF deficits are not universal in ADHD (§2) and lab tests miss real-world executive trouble (§3), neither a poor nor a clean EF test settles anything — reinforcing `hypothesis:no-single-measure-is-diagnostic` and the [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|assessment note]].
- **The lived experience carries more weight than a score.** The felt executive struggles — losing the thread, dropped intentions, the "churning" that derails follow-through — are what the ratings capture and the tests miss, so they belong in an evaluation as reported experience, not as something to be confirmed by a task.
- **EF is a frame, not the diagnosis.** It names *what* is hard (organising, inhibiting, holding a goal) without settling *why* — which still requires ruling out the mimics ([[literature/adhd-and-anxiety|anxiety]], [[literature/adhd-and-sleep|sleep]], [[literature/adhd-and-mood|mood]]) that also degrade executive performance.
