Of the things a person can do without a prescription, exercise has the best evidence in ADHD. It is not a cure and the effect is modest, but it is real, low-risk, and worth building in regardless of how the diagnostic question resolves. This note sets out what the meta-analyses actually show and how big the effect is. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-exercise.jsonl`.

## 1. It improves the core symptoms

The clearest evidence is for aerobic exercise on the defining symptoms. Cerrillo-Urbina et al.'s (2015) meta-analysis of randomised trials found that *"aerobic exercise had a moderate to large effect on core symptoms such as attention (SMD = 0.84), hyperactivity (SMD = 0.56) and impulsivity (SMD = 0.56)"* in children and adolescents (`hypothesis:exercise-reduces-adhd-symptoms`, `intervention:exercise`). The benefit shows up on two timescales. Den Heijer et al. (2017), reviewing the field, report that *"[c]ardio exercise seems acutely beneficial regarding various executive functions (e.g., impulsivity), response time and several physical measures"* — a single bout helps in the following window — and they also found chronic benefits from sustained training. This dovetails with the working-memory finding that movement can *support* cognition in ADHD ([[literature/adhd-and-working-memory|ADHD & working memory]]).

## 2. Modest, and dose-dependent

The honest magnitude is moderate, not transformative. Vysniauske et al.'s (2020) meta-analysis of functional outcomes found *"a significant effect of exercise on ADHD functional outcomes (g = 0.627)"*, and their conclusion sets the expectation: *"exercise has a modest positive impact on ADHD functional outcomes, such as executive functions and motor skills, with longer interventions yielding better results"* (`hypothesis:exercise-benefit-modest-and-dose-dependent`). Two practical points fall out: it is an adjunct, not a substitute for other treatment, and consistency over time matters more than intensity — a sustained habit beats occasional hard sessions.

## 3. Why it plausibly works

The mechanism is not mysterious. Acute aerobic exercise transiently raises catecholamines — dopamine and noradrenaline — in the same direction that stimulant medication does, which is a coherent reason it should touch the attention and inhibition systems central to ADHD ([[literature/adhd-and-motivation|the dopamine-reward account]]). That is a plausibility argument rather than settled mechanism, but it fits both the symptom effects (§1) and the fact that the acute benefit follows a single bout.

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **A no-downside thing to try now.** Unlike a medication trial ([[literature/adhd-interventions|interventions]]), exercise carries no diagnostic ambiguity and broad health benefit, so building in regular aerobic activity is reasonable regardless of what an [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]] concludes — and noticing whether focus is better on days you move is useful information.
- **Set expectations honestly.** §2 says the effect is modest and grows with consistency, so treat it as one supportive lever among several, not a test of whether "it's really ADHD."
- **It is not diagnostic either way.** Responding well to exercise no more confirms ADHD than a good response to a stimulant does — it is a lever that helps attention broadly, so use it as support, not evidence.
