Long before (or instead of) a diagnosis, most people with ADHD build workarounds: lists, alarms, rigid routines, doing the important thing the moment it occurs to them. This note is about those coping strategies — what they are, whether they work, and what they cost. The honest summary: they help, meaningfully, but they are effort-hungry and no substitute for addressing the underlying pattern. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-coping-strategies.jsonl`.

## 1. The strategies people actually use

Coping in ADHD is not one thing. Kreider et al. (2019), studying college students with ADHD and learning disabilities, found their strategies fell into recognisable families: *"[s]trategies classified as habit and routine use, reframing, and symptom-specific strategies were identified."* In plain terms: build external structure and habit so less depends on in-the-moment self-regulation; reframe tasks and setbacks; and target specific symptoms (externalising memory, chunking work, engineering the environment). These map onto the executive weaknesses in [[literature/adhd-and-executive-function|executive function]] — they are prosthetics for the systems that run short.

## 2. Taught as CBT, they work

Coping strategies are not just folk wisdom; taught systematically they have trial evidence. Safren et al.'s (2005) randomised trial added a skills-based CBT — organisation, planning, coping, cognitive restructuring — to stable medication in adults with residual symptoms, and found that *"those who were randomized to CBT had lower IE-rated ADHD symptoms (p < .01) and global severity (p < .002), as well as self-reported ADHD symptoms (p < .0001) than those randomized to continued psychopharmacology alone"* (`hypothesis:coping-skills-reduce-adhd-symptoms`, `intervention:cbt-adult-adhd`). Skills add benefit *on top of* medication — they are a genuine, evidence-based lever (`hypothesis:adhd-treatments-are-efficacious`).

## 3. But it is effortful, and the long-term payoff is uneven

Two caveats keep this honest (`hypothesis:compensation-is-effortful-and-uneven`). First, compensation is expensive: Kreider et al. note that people with ADHD *"must work longer and harder than peers,"* so the same output takes more fuel — a hidden tax that the [[literature/adhd-in-women-and-girls|masking]] literature also describes, and that can quietly exhaust. Second, the benefit is not unlimited: Wettstein et al.'s (2026) long-term follow-up of coping-focused CBT found *"no significant treatment or treatment-by-time effects for the total AAQoL or its subdomains"* (AAQoL being the Adult ADHD Quality of Life scale). Coping skills reduce *symptoms* (§2) more reliably than they lift long-term *quality of life*. That is not a reason to abandon them — it is a reason not to treat "just try harder with better systems" as the whole answer.

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **The workarounds are legitimate, not cheating.** §1–2 say externalising, routine and reframing are evidence-based compensation for real executive weaknesses — the same strategies a clinician would teach. Using them (or building more) is not evading the problem; it is treating it.
- **Externalise to spend less willpower.** The through-line of what works is moving load *out* of the head — into lists, alarms, environment and habit — which is exactly the fix implied by the [[literature/adhd-and-procrastination|procrastination]] and [[literature/adhd-and-working-memory|working-memory]] findings. Systems beat resolve.
- **Notice the effort cost.** §3's "work harder than peers" is worth taking seriously: chronic high-effort compensation is tiring and can mask how much is being carried, which is itself relevant to bring to an [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]] — and a reason the coping is not the whole answer.
