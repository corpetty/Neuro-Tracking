The [[literature/adhd-and-sleep|sleep note]] argued that sleep and ADHD are entangled — mimic, consequence, and shared circadian clock. This note is the actionable other half: if sleep matters that much, does *fixing* it help? The answer is genuinely encouraging — improving sleep improves not just sleep but daytime ADHD symptoms — with one honest caveat about melatonin. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-sleep-hygiene.jsonl`.

## 1. Better sleep, fewer symptoms

The strongest evidence is a randomised trial of a brief behavioural sleep intervention — sleep hygiene plus standard behavioural strategies — in children with ADHD. Hiscock et al. (2015) found that *"[i]ntervention compared with control families reported a greater decrease in ADHD symptoms at three and six months (adjusted mean difference for change in symptom severity -2.9, 95% confidence interval -5.5 to -0.3, P=0.03, effect size -0.3, and -3.7, -6.1 to -1.2, P=0.004, effect size -0.4, respectively)"* (`hypothesis:sleep-intervention-improves-adhd`). It roughly halved moderate-severe sleep problems, and about a third to a half of the *symptom* benefit ran through the improvement in sleep. So this is not just "sleep better and feel less tired" — treating sleep moved the ADHD symptoms themselves (`intervention:sleep-management`).

That is why sleep is the first thing to address. Hvolby (2015) notes that *"[c]urrent guidelines recommend assessment of sleep disturbance during evaluation of ADHD, and before initiation of pharmacotherapy, with healthy sleep practices the first-line option for addressing sleep problems."*

## 2. Melatonin: fixes the clock, not the disorder

For the *delayed* clock common in ADHD ([[literature/adhd-and-sleep|circadian delay]], `mechanism:circadian-delay`), melatonin is the targeted tool. Van der Heijden et al. (2007), in a randomised trial, found *"[s]leep onset advanced by 26.9 +/- 47.8 minutes with melatonin and delayed by 10.5 +/- 37.4 minutes with placebo (p < .0001)"* — a real advance in falling-asleep time. But the important limit: *"[t]here was no significant effect on behavior, cognition, and quality of life, and significant adverse events did not occur"* (`hypothesis:melatonin-advances-sleep-not-symptoms`). Melatonin resets *when* you sleep; it does not, on its own, treat the ADHD. Unlike the behavioural intervention in §1, it is a sleep-timing fix, best used (with a clinician) for a genuine phase delay rather than as an ADHD remedy.

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **A high-value, low-risk lever — try it early.** Given sleep's entanglement with the symptoms, §1 says improving sleep can reduce daytime symptoms regardless of how the [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|diagnostic question]] resolves, and guidelines put it *before* medication. Consistent bed and wake times, a wind-down routine, morning light and dimmer evenings, and limiting evening [[literature/adhd-and-screen-time|screens]] are the concrete pieces.
- **Match the tool to the problem.** If the issue is a *late clock* (can't fall asleep, not short sleep), §2 points to melatonin and light-timing via a clinician; if it is fragmented or short sleep and habits, the behavioural/hygiene route in §1 is first.
- **It doubles as assessment prep.** Characterising and improving sleep is exactly what the [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]] wants done before attributing symptoms — so this is useful whether or not the answer is ADHD.
