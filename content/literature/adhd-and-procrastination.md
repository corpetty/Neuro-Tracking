Ask someone with ADHD what actually derails them and "I put everything off until the last possible second" comes up as often as inattention itself. Procrastination is one of the most recognisable ADHD experiences — and, oddly, one the diagnostic criteria never mention. This note sets out the link and, more usefully, corrects the framing: procrastination is not a time-management flaw to be fixed with a better calendar. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-procrastination.jsonl`.

## 1. Central to ADHD, absent from the criteria

Niermann & Scheres (2014) name the gap directly: procrastination *"is often part of psychotherapies for adults with attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). However, procrastination is officially not acknowledged as an ADHD-related symptom."* It is treated as a core problem clinically while being invisible to the DSM checklist. Where they measured it, the link ran through one domain in particular: *"partial correlations (corrected for the other symptom domain of ADHD) indicated that only inattention was correlated with general procrastination"* (`hypothesis:procrastination-linked-to-adhd`). Not the impulsive "acting without thinking" but the inattentive difficulty engaging and staying with a task.

## 2. It is a self-regulation failure, not poor scheduling

The more important correction is *what kind* of problem procrastination is. Rabin et al. (2011) place it squarely in the executive system: *"[p]rocrastination is increasingly viewed as involving failures in self-regulation and volition, processes commonly regarded as executive functions,"* and their study found executive-function domains predicting academic procrastination (`hypothesis:procrastination-is-self-regulation-failure`). Steel's (2007) meta-analysis of 691 correlations pins down the drivers: *"[s]trong and consistent predictors of procrastination were task aversiveness, task delay, self-efficacy, and impulsiveness, as well as conscientiousness and its facets of self-control, distractibility, organization, and achievement motivation."* Read that list — impulsiveness, distractibility, low self-control, task-delay — and it is essentially the ADHD cognitive profile. Steel adds the theoretical frame: the effects are *"consistent with temporal motivation theory, an integrative hybrid of expectancy theory and hyperbolic discounting"* (`mechanism:reward-delay-aversion`) — the same steep discounting of delayed outcomes that drives ADHD's delay aversion.

So procrastination is the general-population version of a profile ADHD represents at the extreme: it links [[literature/adhd-and-executive-function|executive function]], the [[literature/adhd-and-motivation|reward/delay-discounting]] account, and the sense of [[literature/adhd-and-time-perception|time]] all at once. A better planner does not touch the underlying discounting and task-initiation problem.

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **Reframe it, then it is workable.** §2 says chronic putting-off is a self-regulation and delay-discounting problem, not laziness or a scheduling skill you failed to learn — which points at different strategies (shrinking the delay to reward, reducing task aversiveness, externalising initiation) than yet another to-do system.
- **It is a clue worth naming.** Procrastination is a near-universal ADHD complaint that the criteria omit (§1), so it is exactly the kind of concrete, lifelong pattern worth bringing to an [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]] as experience — while remembering it is common in the general population and so descriptive, not diagnostic.
- **It feeds the self-story.** Repeated last-minute scrambles and missed intentions are a direct input to the accumulated [[literature/adhd-and-self-esteem|low self-esteem]] and guilt this notebook tracks — another reason the reframe from "character flaw" to "self-regulation" matters.
