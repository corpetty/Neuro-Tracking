Diet is the area of ADHD where confident advice most outruns the evidence, so this note is mostly about calibration: which claims are false, which are true-but-small, and what that adds up to. The short version: sugar is not the culprit, a few dietary approaches help a bit for some people, and none of it is a primary treatment. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-diet.jsonl`.

## 1. The sugar myth is a myth

Start with the most entrenched belief, because it is simply wrong. Wolraich et al.'s (1995) meta-analysis of blinded, placebo-controlled sugar-challenge studies concluded that *"sugar does not affect the behavior or cognitive performance of children"* (`hypothesis:sugar-does-not-cause-hyperactivity`). Why does everyone think otherwise? The authors are direct: *"[t]he strong belief of parents may be due to expectancy and common association"* — you notice the wild behaviour at the birthday party and credit the cake, not the excitement. This is a clean example of the notebook's [[literature/adhd-and-rejection-sensitivity|appraise-the-popular-claim]] stance: a near-universal conviction with no controlled support.

## 2. The rest: real but small

Some dietary approaches do help, modestly, for some people (`hypothesis:dietary-effects-on-adhd-are-small`, `intervention:dietary-modification`). Nigg et al.'s (2012) meta-analysis found that *"[r]estriction diets reduced ADHD symptoms at an effect of g = 0.29 (95% CI, 0.07-0.53),"* concluding a restriction diet benefits *some* children. For synthetic food colours the effect is smaller and shakier: *"[f]or food colors, parent reports yielded an effect size of g = 0.18 (95% CI, 0.08-0.24; p = .0007), which decreased to 0.12 (95% CI, 0.01-0.23; p < .05) after adjustment for possible publication bias."* And omega-3 supplementation has the most consistent small signal — Bloch & Qawasmi (2011) found that *"[o]mega-3 fatty acid supplementation, particularly with higher doses of eicosapentaenoic acid, was modestly effective in the treatment of ADHD,"* while noting it is less effective than standard medication.

## 3. What it adds up to

Three practical conclusions. First, no dietary intervention is a primary treatment — the effect sizes are a fraction of medication's (compare [[literature/adhd-interventions|interventions]]), and diet belongs alongside [[literature/adhd-and-exercise|exercise]] as a low-risk adjunct. Second, the ones with any support target subgroups: an elimination diet helps *some* children, not all, so a personal trial (done carefully, ideally with guidance) tells you more than the average does. Third, the food-additive story is real but marginal and bias-prone, and the sugar story is not real at all.

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **Drop the sugar theory.** §1 says fluctuations in focus or restlessness after eating are not a sugar effect; chasing it is a distraction from the actual differential.
- **Diet is optional adjunct, not answer.** Omega-3 is benign and modestly supported, and a generally healthy diet is reasonable on its own merits — but §2 sets expectations low, and none of it bears on whether the underlying pattern is ADHD.
- **A dietary response would not be diagnostic anyway.** As with exercise and medication, feeling a bit better on a dietary change is not evidence about the diagnosis — it is a small lever, weighed as support, decided clinically at an [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]].
