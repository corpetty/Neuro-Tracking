There is a particular flavour of doubt that shadows ADHD: not "is this hard?" but "is this *real*, or am I just making excuses?" That doubt is not only private — it is the core of how ADHD is stigmatised, and many people with it absorb the stigma inward. This note documents both, and points out that the doubt at its centre is a factual claim the rest of this notebook has already tested. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-stigma.jsonl`.

## 1. The stigma is real and lifelong

People with ADHD are stigmatised, across ages and settings. Mueller et al.'s (2012) review concluded that *"patients suffering from ADHD are at high risk to be confronted with stigma, prejudices, and discrimination."* Lebowitz's (2016) developmental review, drawing on nationally representative, experimental, survey and qualitative evidence, found that *"individuals of all ages who exhibit symptoms of ADHD are the recipients of substantial stigmatization"* (`hypothesis:adhd-carries-substantial-stigma`). This is not a fringe attitude; it is present throughout development.

## 2. Its engine is doubt about validity

What is the stigma *about*? Repeatedly, it is disbelief that ADHD is a genuine condition. Masuch et al. (2019), surveying adults with ADHD, found that the *"[m]ost frequently perceived stereotypes were doubts about the validity of ADHD as a mental disorder."* Mueller et al. likewise identify public uncertainty about the reliability and validity of the diagnosis as a leading contributor. That matters here for a specific reason: validity is not a matter of opinion, and it is exactly what the rest of this notebook examines. The [[literature/dimensional-continuum-adhd|dimensional and continuum evidence]] shows ADHD is the heritable extreme of a continuously distributed trait; the [[literature/adhd-neurobiology-imaging|neurobiology]] shows real (if small) brain differences. The stigma's central premise — "it's not real" — is the one claim the evidence directly refutes.

## 3. It gets internalized

Stigma does not stay external. Masuch et al. (2019) found it common inside as well: *"[t]he sample comprised n = 104 adults with ADHD, of whom n = 24 (23.3%) reported high internalized stigma, n = 92 (88.5%) anticipated discrimination in daily life and n = 70 (69.3%) perceived public stigma."* And it has a cost: *"[i]nternalized stigma and anticipated discrimination are highly prevalent in adult ADHD and correlate with the burden of disease"* (`hypothesis:internalized-stigma-adds-burden`) — correlating, in their data, with distress, lower self-esteem and reduced quality of life. The public "it's just an excuse" becomes the private "I'm just making excuses," feeding the [[literature/adhd-and-self-esteem|self-esteem]] cycle this notebook already tracks (`mechanism:low-self-esteem`).

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **The self-doubt is not neutral evidence — it is stigma.** "Am I just making excuses?" feels like honest self-scrutiny but is, in large part, internalised public stigma (§3). Recognising it as such is not permission to stop questioning; it is a reason not to let the doubt itself count as an answer.
- **The validity question is answerable, and answered.** §2's core stereotype — that ADHD is not real — is not a standoff of opinions; the dimensional, genetic and neurobiological evidence settles the "is it real" question in the affirmative, whatever an individual's [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]] concludes about *them*.
- **Taking it seriously is not self-indulgence.** Building this record and pursuing an assessment is the opposite of making excuses — it is the careful scrutiny stigma pretends to value. The burden documented in §3 is a reason to address the question properly, not to dismiss it.
