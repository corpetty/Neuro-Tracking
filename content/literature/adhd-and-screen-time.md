Few worries about ADHD are as loud as screens — the assumption that phones and fast-paced media are causing, or at least worsening, attention problems. The evidence is real but much smaller and more ambiguous than the worry, and the direction of the arrow is the crux. This note lays out both. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-screen-time.jsonl`.

## 1. The association is small

The link exists, but it is modest. Nikkelen et al.'s (2014) meta-analysis of 45 studies found *"a small significant relationship between media use and ADHD-related behaviors (r+ = .12)."* The four-decade review by Beyens et al. (2018) reached the same magnitude: *"[t]he available evidence suggests a statistically small relationship between media and ADHD-related behaviors."* Even the most-cited prospective study points the same way — Ra et al. (2018), following 15- and 16-year-olds over two years, found that *"there was a statistically significant but modest association between higher frequency of digital media use and subsequent symptoms of ADHD"* (`hypothesis:screen-media-adhd-association-is-small`). Small, consistent, and a long way from the strong effect popular framing assumes.

## 2. Which way does the arrow point?

The harder question is causality, and here the honest answer is "unknown" (`hypothesis:screen-adhd-causality-unresolved`). Ra et al. (2018) — a *prospective* design, the kind best placed to argue causation — still concluded that *"[f]urther research is needed to determine whether this association is causal,"* and Beyens et al. (2018) note that *"the extent to which screen media use and ADHD are linked remains a point of debate."* Reverse causation is entirely plausible: the reward-seeking and need for stimulation of ADHD ([[literature/adhd-and-motivation|the reward-motivation account]], `mechanism:reward-motivation`) make intensely stimulating digital media especially attractive, so heavy screen use may be an *expression* of the trait as much as a driver of it. The correlation is compatible with screens feeding symptoms, symptoms feeding screen use, or a shared third factor.

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **Don't over-attribute the symptoms to screens.** §1 says the association is small, so heavy screen use is unlikely to be *the* explanation for an attention pattern — and blaming screens can distract from the real differential.
- **Heavy use may be a clue, not a cause.** §2's reverse-causation reading means a strong pull toward stimulating media can itself be part of the picture (reward-seeking), worth noticing rather than only policing.
- **Manage it where it clearly bites — sleep.** The cleanest reason to limit screens is not ADHD causation but the knock-on effects, especially on [[literature/adhd-and-sleep|sleep]], which does feed daytime symptoms. That is a concrete lever independent of the unresolved causal question, and none of it bears on the [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|diagnosis]] itself.
