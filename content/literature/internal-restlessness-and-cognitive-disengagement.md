The core symptom in this notebook is a *disruptive internal state* — outwardly calm, inwardly churning ([[about-me/symptoms|symptom log]]). The open question `question:what-is-the-internal-state` asks what that state actually *is*: racing thoughts, a wound-up physical-but-invisible feeling, an inability to ever relax, or something else. Those descriptions feel similar from the inside but point in different directions. This note brings two research constructs to bear on the question, because each has a measurement instrument and a different differential attached. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/internal-restlessness-cds.jsonl`.

The two constructs sit at **opposite ends of an arousal axis**. One is hyper-aroused and restless; the other is hypo-aroused and foggy. Deciding which fits is not academic — they route to different rivals (`tension:internal-restlessness-vs-cognitive-disengagement`).

## 1. The restless pole: internalized hyperactivity

The DSM has long conceded that adult hyperactivity need not look like a fidgeting child. Weyandt et al. (2003), developing the **Internal Restlessness Scale (IRS)**, put the point plainly: *"hyperactivity in adolescents and adults may be limited to subjective feelings of restlessness. Recent studies with adults with ADHD have also reported that mental restlessness is commonly reported by individuals with the disorder."* Their scale exists precisely to measure that inner experience rather than observable motion, and it discriminates: *"college students with ADHD report significantly higher ratings of internal restlessness than college students without ADHD."* The IRS resolves the construct into four factors — internal distractibility, internal impulsivity, internal restlessness, and internal disorganization — so "churning inside" is not one thing but a small family of them.

A clinician-facing review reaches the same place from the management side. Weibel et al. (2020) note that in adults *"hyperactivity is more often internalized, symptoms of inattention may be masked by anxiety symptoms or obsessive-like compensation strategies."* That single sentence does double duty here: it supports reading the internal state as the adult form of the hyperactive axis (`hypothesis:internal-restlessness-is-internalized-hyperactivity`), and in the same breath it names why this pole is so hard to adjudicate — internalized hyperactivity and anxious arousal are near-indistinguishable from the inside, which is exactly the live `tension:adhd-vs-anxiety-internal-restlessness`.

**What this pole implies.** If the internal state is restless, racing, wound-up — a motor running with no outlet — then it is the hyperactivity/impulsivity axis experienced mentally, and the primary rival to rule out is **anxiety**.

## 2. The foggy pole: cognitive disengagement syndrome

There is a second, opposite reading, and it has moved from the fringe to a named research construct in the last decade. What used to be called *sluggish cognitive tempo* (SCT) was renamed by a 2023 work-group consensus to **cognitive disengagement syndrome (CDS)**. Becker et al. (2023) define its cognitive core as *"cognitive symptoms involving the disengagement or decoupling of attention and conscious or effortful mental processing from the ongoing external context, as reflected in difficulties with staring, daydreaming, mental confusion or fogginess, withdrawal, and sleepy appearance."* This is not restlessness. It is the mind drifting *offline* — foggy, slow, disengaged.

The reason it matters for the differential is that CDS is **statistically separable from ADHD inattention** and leans toward a different comorbidity. Becker et al.'s (2016) meta-analysis, across factor-analytic studies of more than 19,000 individuals, concluded that *"SCT is a distinct symptom dimension that is separable from ADHD and other dimensions of psychopathology."* Its correlate profile places it near inattention but pulled toward mood: *"SCT is more strongly associated with ADHD inattention (r = 0.63 in children, r = 0.72 in adults) than with ADHD hyperactivity-impulsivity (r = 0.32 in children, r = 0.46 in adults), and it likewise appears that SCT is more strongly associated with internalizing symptoms than with externalizing symptoms."* The 2023 consensus restates the distinctness in its own words: *"SCT and ADHD-IN are empirically distinct and correlated more strongly (meta-analytic rs = .63 and .72 in children and adults, respectively) than SCT and ADHD-HI (meta-analytic rs = .32 and .46 in children and adults, respectively)."*

**What this pole implies.** If the internal state is foggy, drifting, and mentally slow rather than restless, then `hypothesis:cds-distinct-from-inattention` says the better description may be cognitive disengagement — and its strongest tie is to **depression / internalizing**, moving the primary rival from anxiety to **mood**.

## 3. Why holding both open is the point

These two constructs are not rival theories to pick between in the abstract; they are two different *phenomenologies*, and only concrete observation of the internal state can say which is present — possibly both, at different times. That is the tension this note reifies:

| | Restless pole | Foggy pole |
|---|---|---|
| Construct | Internalized hyperactivity (IRS) | Cognitive disengagement syndrome (CDS/SCT) |
| Arousal | Hyper — racing, wound-up | Hypo — slow, drifting, staring |
| ADHD axis | Hyperactivity/impulsivity, experienced mentally | Separable from inattention; not the HI axis |
| Nearest rival to rule out | Anxiety | Depression / low mood |
| Source | Weyandt 2003; Weibel 2020 | Becker 2016, 2023 |

The honest caveats travel with both. The IRS evidence is a small college sample, and Weibel's own point is that this presentation is where anxiety hides. CDS is a real but still-consolidating construct — the 2023 group itself flags that the *name* will keep evolving, and adult data remain thinner than child data. Neither construct is a diagnosis, and the record does not yet contain the concrete self-observation needed to say which pole is in play.

## Where this leaves the personal case

The practical payoff is a sharper journaling target for `question:what-is-the-internal-state`. Instead of "is the internal state disruptive," the question becomes: **is it restless or foggy?** — because the answer changes which rival matters most. When the state is churning and racing, look toward anxiety; when it is drifting and mentally slow, look toward mood. Bringing both constructs, and the observation of which one fits, to a clinician is more useful than reporting "internal restlessness" alone. This connects back to the mechanism-level differential developed in [[dimensions/arousal-and-mind-wandering|Arousal, state regulation, and mind-wandering]] and to the load-bearing personal `hypothesis:adhd-explains-internal-restlessness`, which both of these mechanisms now sit beside as rivals.
