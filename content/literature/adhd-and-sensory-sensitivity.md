Sensitivity to sound, light, texture and touch — being overwhelmed by a noisy room, unable to ignore a scratchy label, or conversely craving strong sensory input — is usually filed under autism. But it shows up in ADHD too, and the recent evidence treats it as part of the ADHD picture in its own right. This note lays out what is known and how tentative it still is. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-sensory-sensitivity.jsonl`.

## 1. Elevated, in both directions

Sensory atypicality in ADHD runs both ways — over-responsive *and* under-responsive. Bijlenga et al. (2017), studying 116 adults with ADHD, found that *"[a]dults with ADHD had more autistic symptoms, and they had both more hyper- and hyposensitivity compared to norm groups"* (`mechanism:sensory-sensitivity`), most notably in the auditory and activity-level domains. The pattern is not confined to adults: Ghanizadeh's (2011) systematic review of children concluded that *"[s]ensory processing problems in children with ADHD are more common than in typically developing children"* (`hypothesis:sensory-sensitivity-elevated-in-adhd`).

## 2. It scales with the trait

Consistent with the continuum view this notebook keeps returning to, sensory difficulty tracks ADHD *dimensionally*, not just categorically. Panagiotidi et al. (2018), sampling the general population, found *"a highly significant positive correlation between the number of ADHD traits and the frequency of reported sensory processing problems"* — more ADHD traits, more sensory difficulty, across all modalities. Bijlenga et al. similarly reported a dose-response relationship with ADHD severity. This mirrors the [[literature/dimensional-continuum-adhd|dimensional models]]: the feature is graded, not present-or-absent.

## 3. Not merely autism comorbidity — but hold it lightly

The obvious objection is that sensory issues are really autism, showing up because ADHD and autism co-occur ([[literature/autism-and-adhd|the overlap]]). Bijlenga et al. addressed this directly, and the title carries the finding: *"Atypical sensory profiles as core features of adult ADHD, irrespective of autistic symptoms"* (`hypothesis:sensory-difficulties-part-of-adhd-phenotype-not-just-autism`). Panagiotidi et al. go a cautious step further: *"[o]ur results suggest that sensory difficulties could be part of the ADHD phenotype."*

The caution is warranted. Ghanizadeh's review is blunt that *"[s]ensory processing problems in children with ADHD is not a well studied area,"* and the evidence is largely questionnaire-based. And sensory sensitivity is plainly *not specific* to ADHD — it is central to autism and appears in anxiety and sensory processing conditions besides. So the honest reading is: a real, replicated, graded feature of ADHD that is nonetheless emerging and shared.

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **A recognised part of the picture, if present.** If noisy or overstimulating environments are draining or derailing, or if certain textures/sounds are hard to filter out, that is consistent with ADHD and worth noting at an [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]] — not a fringe complaint.
- **It can feed the restlessness.** Sensory overload is a plausible driver of distractibility and of the internal churn (`symptom:internal-restlessness`), so sensory context is worth tracking alongside the internal-state question rather than treated separately.
- **Describes, does not diagnose.** Because sensory sensitivity is shared with autism and others (§3), its presence supports the broader phenotype without confirming ADHD — one more transdiagnostic strand, weighed clinically, not a test.
