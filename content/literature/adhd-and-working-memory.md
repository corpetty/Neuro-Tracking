Working memory — holding and manipulating information "in mind" over seconds — is where a lot of ADHD's day-to-day trouble lives: losing the thread mid-sentence, forgetting the second half of an instruction, walking into a room and not knowing why. It is also one of the disorder's most solidly established cognitive findings, and it comes with a genuinely surprising twist about movement. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-working-memory.jsonl`.

## 1. A large, robust, lasting deficit

Working memory sits near the centre of cognitive models of ADHD. Kasper et al. (2012) note that *"[w]orking memory has assumed a prominent role as a primary neurocognitive deficit or endophenotype in extant models of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD),"* and their meta-analysis found *"statistically significant, large magnitude working memory deficits relative to their typically developing peers"* (`mechanism:working-memory`). The deficit is not uniform across types: Martinussen et al. (2005) found *"[o]verall effect sizes for spatial storage (effect size = 0.85, CI = 0.62 - 1.08) and spatial central executive WM (effect size = 1.06, confidence interval = 0.72-1.39) were greater than those obtained for verbal storage (effect size = 0.47, confidence interval = 0.36-0.59) and verbal central executive WM (effect size = 0.43, confidence interval = 0.24-0.62)"* — spatial working memory is hit harder than verbal.

Two features make it robust (`hypothesis:wm-deficit-central-and-persistent`). It is not an artefact of other conditions — Martinussen et al. found the deficits *"independent of comorbidity with language learning disorders and weaknesses in general intellectual ability"* — and it does not resolve with age. Alderson et al.'s (2013) adult meta-analysis concluded that *"WM deficits persist into adulthood and suggest that methodological variability may explicate why WM deficits have not been uniformly detected in previous experimental studies."* This connects directly to the executive-function account, where working memory is one of the abilities Barkley's model derives from inhibition ([[literature/adhd-and-executive-function|ADHD & executive function]]).

## 2. The twist: movement may help

Here is the counterintuitive part. The restless movement that defines hyperactivity — usually treated as a symptom to suppress — may partly be the brain recruiting the body to support working memory. Sarver et al. (2015) measured gross motor activity during working-memory tasks and found that *"higher rates of activity level predicted significantly better, but not normalized WM performance for children with ADHD. Conversely, higher rates of activity level predicted somewhat lower WM performance for TD children"* (TD = typically developing). Movement helped the ADHD group and hindered the comparison group.

That reframes the fidgeting, pacing and "driven by a motor" restlessness (`tension:hyperactivity-deficit-or-compensation`): at least some of it may be compensatory, recruited when working-memory demands are high, rather than pure noise. It is a live tension — the same activity can also impair — but it means telling someone with ADHD to "sit still and concentrate" may be working against the very thing helping them hold the task in mind.

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **A concrete, testable deficit — and a concrete experience to report.** Losing the thread and dropping mid-task intentions are the everyday face of a well-replicated working-memory deficit; they are worth bringing to an [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]] as specific examples, not vague forgetfulness.
- **The restlessness may be doing something.** If the internal restlessness or a need to move rises exactly when trying to hold something in mind, §2 suggests that is not necessarily a problem to eliminate — it may be a coping strategy. This bears on the internal-state question rather than settling it.
- **Still one pathway, not a test.** Working-memory deficits are central but not universal in ADHD and not unique to it (`hypothesis:multiple-neurocognitive-pathways`), and a working-memory task is not a diagnostic instrument — it describes the difficulty, it does not confirm the cause.
