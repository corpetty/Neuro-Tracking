Few ADHD ideas have spread as fast online as "rejection sensitive dysphoria" (RSD) — an extreme emotional reaction to perceived rejection or criticism. It resonates, and it is worth taking seriously. But this notebook's job is to separate what is felt from what is established, and RSD is a case where a popular label runs ahead of its evidence. This note keeps both in view: the real, validated construct underneath, and the honest limits of the ADHD-specific claim. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-rejection-sensitivity.jsonl`.

## 1. The validated construct: rejection sensitivity

Underneath the label is a genuine, decades-old psychological construct. Downey & Feldman (1996) defined rejection sensitivity as a general cognitive-affective disposition: *"People who are sensitive to social rejection tend to anxiously expect, readily perceive, and overreact to it"* (`mechanism:rejection-sensitivity`). Crucially, this is *not* ADHD-specific — it was developed studying intimate relationships and is documented across depression, anxiety and personality disorders. So the *experience* RSD describes is real and measurable; it simply predates and extends beyond ADHD.

## 2. The popular label: coined from anecdote, thin on evidence

"Rejection sensitive dysphoria" is a different thing from the construct: a specific ADHD framing that spread through social media. van Asselt's (2026) critical reflection in the Dutch journal of psychiatry traces its origin plainly — *"American psychiatrist William Dodson introduced the concept based on clinical experience with clients with ADHD."* That is clinical anecdote, not a validation study, and the appraisal is candid about the cost of adopting it uncritically: its clinical use *"entails four risks: 1. an increase in (self-)stigmatisation; 2. the framing as innate, causing social-environmental influences to remain underemphasised; 3. terminological fragmentation and confusion; and 4. limited empirical evidence"* (`hypothesis:rsd-is-under-evidenced-popular-label`). The recommended fix is not to discard the experience but to name it with the established construct — the rejection-sensitivity spectrum — rather than a new, unvalidated syndrome.

## 3. What the ADHD-specific evidence actually is

Between the solid general construct and the shaky syndrome label sits the real question: how strong is the evidence that rejection sensitivity is elevated *in ADHD*? Honestly, thin and early. It is usually placed under emotional dysregulation, which *is* well established in ADHD — Shaw et al. (2014) found *"emotion dysregulation is prevalent in ADHD throughout the lifespan and is a major contributor to impairment."* But rejection sensitivity as a distinct ADHD feature is barely studied: Rowney-Smith et al. (2026) note that *"[a]n aspect of emotional dysregulation which remains relatively unexplored in ADHD, is rejection sensitivity. In people who experience rejection sensitivity, perceived rejection and/or criticism can evoke extreme dysphoria"* — and their own study is five undergraduates in a focus group (`hypothesis:rejection-sensitivity-part-of-adhd-ED`). Plausible and clinically resonant; not yet firmly established, and not specific to ADHD.

## 4. The tension worth holding

This leaves a real pull in two directions (`tension:rsd-validation-vs-rigor`). The RSD label gives many people a sense of recognition and connectedness — a genuine clinical good, and reason not to simply dismiss it. Yet the same appraisal that grants this warns it can reify a trait as innate, add stigma, and rest on little evidence. Both are true at once. The honest position is neither to embrace RSD as an ADHD hallmark nor to wave the experience away, but to treat the *phenomenon* as real emotional-dysregulation-linked rejection sensitivity while treating the *specific syndrome claim* as unproven.

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **It is a description, not a diagnosis.** Even if rejection sensitivity fits, it is a transdiagnostic feature (§1), so it neither confirms ADHD nor distinguishes it from the mimics already tracked here — [[literature/adhd-and-anxiety|anxiety]] and [[literature/adhd-and-mood|mood]] carry rejection sensitivity too. It belongs under emotional dysregulation, developed in [[literature/adhd-and-anxiety|ADHD & anxiety]].
- **Beware the innate framing.** The "hardwired RSD" story can crowd out situational and mood-related contributions (van Asselt's risk 2); if rejection-related pain is prominent, that is a reason to look at context and mood, not to close the question.
- **Language for a clinician, used carefully.** If this pattern is part of the internal state, naming it as *rejection sensitivity* (the established term) rather than *RSD* keeps the conversation on evidenced ground while still capturing the experience.
