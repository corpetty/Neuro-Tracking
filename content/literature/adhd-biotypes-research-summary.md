## The Science Behind "3 Types of ADHD": What the New Brain-Imaging Study Actually Found

## TL;DR
- The National Geographic post is based on a real, peer-reviewed study — Pan, Long, Qin, et al., "Mapping ADHD Heterogeneity and Biotypes by Topological Deviations in Morphometric Similarity Networks," published in *JAMA Psychiatry* (online February 25, 2026; print May 2026, 83(5):478–490; DOI 10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2026.0001; PMID 41739459), with corresponding authors Ying Chen, MD, PhD and Qiyong Gong, MD, PhD of the Department of Radiology, West China Hospital of Sichuan University, Chengdu, plus collaborators at the University of Cincinnati (US) and Monash University's Turner Institute in Clayton, Australia. It used structural MRI and machine learning to identify three neurobiological "biotypes" of ADHD.
- The three biotypes are: (1) severe-combined with emotional dysregulation (medial prefrontal cortex–pallidum alterations), (2) predominantly hyperactive/impulsive (anterior cingulate cortex–pallidum), and (3) predominantly inattentive (superior frontal gyrus). Notably, these brain-derived groups broadly aligned with the long-standing DSM presentations even though no symptom data guided the clustering — and the core findings replicated in an independent cohort.
- The findings are scientifically credible but clinically immature: this is not a diagnostic test, cannot classify an individual patient, and does not currently change treatment. The Instagram framing ("There might be 3 different types of ADHD") is reasonable and appropriately softened by "might" and "suggests"; but some secondary coverage overstated the treatment-response and genetic angles.

## Key Findings

**The study is correctly identified.** The NatGeo article ("There might be 3 different types of ADHD, new brain study suggests," by Helen Bradshaw, published March 3, 2026) links directly to the *JAMA Psychiatry* paper. The image credit (Zephyr/Science Photo Library, a colorized 3D MRI of a child's brain) and the "three biotypes / brain imaging / trial-and-error treatment" framing all match. Quoted researchers include co-author Melissa P. DelBello (University of Cincinnati) and co-author Manpreet K. Singh (UC Davis), plus independent commentators Steven Pliszka (UT Health San Antonio) and Stephen Faraone (SUNY Upstate).

**Methods (brain-first, not symptom-first).** Researchers analyzed structural (T1-weighted) MRI. From each child's scan they built a "morphometric similarity network" — a map of how ~90 brain regions resemble one another in structural features (cortical thickness, surface area, curvature, etc.). They computed three network "hubness" metrics (degree centrality, nodal efficiency, participation coefficient), then built normative models — essentially brain-network "growth charts" from typically developing children — to quantify how much each child with ADHD deviated from expected values for their age and sex. Finally they applied HYDRA, a semi-supervised clustering algorithm, which separates cases from controls with multiple hyperplanes, each facet defining a distinct biotype. The **discovery cohort was 446 children with ADHD and 708 typically developing controls (1,154 total)** from multiple sites in China and the US; the HYDRA clustering assigned 142, 177, and 127 children to Biotypes 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Results were **validated in an independent cohort of 554 children with ADHD and 123 controls from the Healthy Brain Network**. Total across cohorts was roughly 1,831 participants — which is why coverage variously cites "446," "over 1,150," "over 1,800," or "over 3,500" (the 3,500 figure appears to count all scans/controls broadly).

**A shared anchor plus three distinct profiles.** Across all children with ADHD, the orbitofrontal cortex emerged as a common network "anchor point" of dysfunction that transcended biotype boundaries. The three biotypes then differed in where else their networks deviated:
- **Biotype 1 — Severe-combined with emotional dysregulation.** The most widespread deviations (roughly 45 affected regions versus about 26 and 11 for the others), centered on medial prefrontal cortex–pallidum circuits ("an overloaded control center," per Singh). Highest inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity scores, plus pronounced, persistent emotional dysregulation over a 4-year follow-up. Independent expert Pliszka noted this group is most at risk for later depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, substance abuse, and criminality — and authors suggest it needs the earliest intervention. Its deviation map spatially corresponded to serotonin, dopamine, and acetylcholine receptor systems.
- **Biotype 2 — Predominantly hyperactive/impulsive.** Alterations concentrated in anterior cingulate cortex–pallidum circuits involved in error monitoring and behavioral "braking" ("an impulse circuit jam"). Lower inattention than the others; clinically presents as restlessness, blurting, interrupting, acting before thinking. Deviation map linked to glutamate and cannabinoid receptor distributions.
- **Biotype 3 — Predominantly inattentive.** More focal alterations in the superior frontal gyrus, which supports working memory and sustained attention ("everything else works, but focus drifts"). Subtler presentation, more often overlooked, seen more frequently in girls per Pliszka. Deviation map linked to a specific serotonin receptor subtype (5-HT2A).

**Replication and validity signals.** The three-biotype solution was reproduced in the independent Healthy Brain Network cohort, with the hyperactivity/impulsivity gradient (Biotype 1 > 2 > 3) consistent across cohorts. Because a purely brain-based clustering (no symptom input) produced groups that map onto recognizable clinical patterns, the authors argue the biotypes reflect genuine neurobiology rather than noise — and, interestingly, provide the first biological validation of the DSM presentations. That said, effect sizes were modest (e.g., the three-cluster solution had an adjusted Rand Index around 0.21, and symptom differences across biotypes had p-values in the .01–.05 range).

## Details

**Prior context — this is part of a wave, not a bolt from the blue.** ADHD has long been split into DSM "presentations": predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, and combined (formerly "subtypes," renamed "presentations" in DSM-5 because they shift over time). Those categories were built from behavioral checklists. The new study's novelty is deriving subgroups from neurobiology first and finding they line up with the clinical picture. It parallels other recent "biotype" work: Tozzi, Zhang, Pines, et al. (senior author Leanne M. Williams), "Personalized brain circuit scores identify clinically distinct biotypes in depression and anxiety," *Nature Medicine* 2024;30:2076–2087 (published June 17, 2024), identified six biotypes of depression/anxiety from fMRI in 801 treatment-free patients (with 250 randomized to therapy or medication). And a separate ADHD study — Yiwei Chen, Mingyang Li, et al. (Zhejiang University), "Distinct neuroimaging subtypes of ADHD among adolescents based on semi-supervised learning," *Translational Psychiatry* 2025 Nov 17;15(1):476 (DOI 10.1038/s41398-025-03662-3) — used cortical thickness in 6,509 adolescents from the ABCD cohort to find "three distinct subtypes of ADHD with abnormal cortical thickness (CT)… under-developed (lower CT), over-developed (higher CT), and mixed subtypes." The convergence of two different ADHD methods on a three-way split strengthens the case that ADHD is biologically heterogeneous — though the specific subgroup definitions differ between the two studies (network topology versus cortical thickness).

**Important corrections to secondary coverage.** Some outlets (notably Technology Networks) reported that the biotypes "responded differently to stimulant medications" and were linked to "gene expression through spatial transcriptomics." Based on the study's own text, these appear to be journalistic over-extrapolations. The paper did not run a medication trial or test response to a named drug like methylphenidate, and it did not perform a transcriptomic (named-gene) analysis. What it actually did was (a) correlate biotype brain-deviation maps with PET-derived neurotransmitter receptor density maps (spatial, exploratory correlations across 19 receptor maps), and (b) track symptom trajectories over 4 years. On trajectories: symptom improvement over time was broadly parallel across biotypes, but Biotype 1 retained significantly more emotional-dysregulation problems than Biotypes 2 and 3 (Z ≈ −2.1 and −2.7), and had a numerically higher rate of mood-disorder comorbidity during follow-up (25.0% vs. 9.8% vs. 5.0%) that did not reach statistical significance. The "trial-and-error treatment" framing in the headline is thus an aspiration the authors point toward, not a demonstrated result.

**What the authors themselves stress.** Per NatGeo, the authors say the biotypes are "not new diagnostic categories," the findings "will need replication," and ADHD "remains a clinical diagnosis based on behavioral evaluation — not brain scans." DelBello framed the result as confirming what clinicians already do: "it's really nice to have the data to show that our clinical impressions … have some biological validity." Faraone cautioned "there's no perfect prediction algorithm for ADHD. I've never seen one."

**Independent expert critique.** Columbia psychiatrist Ryan Sultan, in a detailed May 2026 analysis, places the study in the "scientifically credible, clinically immature" category: large multi-site sample, top journal, novel method, but a single study not yet independently replicated by other groups, and — crucially — a group-level statistical finding, not an individual-level test. He stresses that no brain scan (MRI, fMRI, SPECT, PET, EEG, qEEG) currently diagnoses ADHD or identifies a person's biotype with clinical accuracy, and warns specifically against commercial SPECT "ADHD type" scans — singling out Amen Clinics, which markets seven SPECT-based ADHD "types" with out-of-pocket scans "typically at a cost of $3,000-$4,500 or more," a practice on a far weaker evidence base than the research studies. He notes the American Psychiatric Association's Council on Children, Adolescents, and Their Families concluded in a 2005 white paper that "the available evidence does not support the use of brain imaging for clinical diagnosis or treatment of psychiatric disorders in children and adolescents" — a position that has not changed. The ADHD Evidence Project (Faraone's group, March 31, 2026) similarly cautioned that "the neurochemical associations reported are exploratory and spatial in nature… and do not directly establish that any particular neurotransmitter system is altered in each subtype, nor do they currently inform treatment decisions," and that strict comorbidity exclusions and US/China-only data limit generalizability.

**Limitations in brief.** Single study (though internally replicated); samples not fully medication-naive; strict comorbidity exclusions unlike real-world clinics; data only from the US and China; biotypes likely represent regions along a continuum rather than hard categorical boundaries; and effect sizes for the clustering and symptom differences were modest.

## Recommendations
- **If you're a curious reader:** Treat this as an exciting research advance in understanding *why* ADHD looks so different person-to-person — not as a new way to get diagnosed. The most defensible takeaway is that ADHD's heterogeneity has a detectable biological structure that roughly tracks the familiar inattentive/hyperactive/combined picture, giving the DSM presentations their first real biological validation.
- **If you or your child has ADHD:** Do not seek a brain scan (MRI or SPECT) to determine "your ADHD type." Diagnosis remains clinical — developmental history, validated rating scales from multiple informants, and functional-impairment assessment. Medication selection is still partly trial-and-error; that is expected, not a failure of diagnosis. Skip qEEG and pharmacogenomic testing as part of an initial workup too.
- **What would change these recommendations:** Watch for (1) independent replication by other research groups (not just the authors' internal validation), (2) prospective studies showing a biotype actually *predicts* which medication works, and (3) demonstration that individual patients (not just group averages) can be reliably assigned to a biotype. Sultan estimates biotype-guided care is plausibly a 2030s development, not a 2026 one.

## Caveats
- The paywalled NatGeo article's full text was accessible for this report, so quotes and framing above are drawn directly from it; its publication date is March 3, 2026 (the Instagram post you saw appears to be a later re-share of the same article).
- Sample-size figures vary across coverage (446 / ~1,150 / ~1,800 / ~3,500) depending on whether one counts the ADHD discovery group only, discovery plus controls, or both cohorts; the paper's discovery cohort is 446 ADHD + 708 controls, with a 554-ADHD validation cohort.
- Claims in some outlets about differential stimulant response and specific gene expression are **not** supported by the study's actual analyses and should be disregarded as overreach; the real analyses were spatial correlations with PET neurotransmitter maps and 4-year symptom trajectories.
- The JAMA paper's full text is paywalled; the figures and statistics here are corroborated across the abstract, the authors' own published Q&A, the ADHD Evidence Project write-up, the Brain & Behavior Research Foundation summary, and the study's preprint (medRxiv 2025.03.27.25324802).
