The [[literature/adhd-in-women-and-girls|women-and-girls note]] introduced one strand of this — that ADHD symptoms can move with the menstrual cycle. This note develops the wider picture: sex hormones do not just colour ADHD at one point in the month but interact with it across the whole female lifespan, and that interaction is one of the more neglected areas of ADHD research. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-hormones.jsonl`.

## 1. A plausible mechanism: hormones act on the same systems ADHD does

The link is not coincidental. Haimov-Kochman & Berger (2014) point to the overlap at the level of neural signalling: *"[t]here is a growing body of literature showing that sex hormones have the ability to regulate intracellular signaling systems that are thought to be abnormal in ADHD"* (`mechanism:reproductive-hormone-modulation`). Estrogen in particular influences dopamine, the neurotransmitter most implicated in ADHD, so it is coherent that *"this functional interaction between sex hormones and molecules involved with synaptic plasticity and neurotransmitter systems may be associated with some of the clinical characteristics of women with ADHD."* When estrogen falls, the systems ADHD already taxes get less support.

## 2. Across the lifespan, not just the month

The consequence is a lifelong pattern of fluctuation. Kooij et al.'s (2025) review is titled for exactly this: *"Research advances and future directions in female ADHD: the lifelong interplay of hormonal fluctuations with mood, cognition, and disease"* (`hypothesis:sex-hormones-modulate-adhd-across-lifespan`). Within the month, Roberts et al. (2018) had already shown that *"ADHD symptoms may change across the menstrual cycle in response to endogenous steroid changes."* Extend that across puberty, pregnancy and postpartum, hormonal contraception, and the estrogen decline of perimenopause, and symptom severity has a moving hormonal backdrop that a single clinic visit — or a male-referenced model — will not see.

## 3. Elevated hormone-related mood disorders

The sensitivity is not only about attention; it shows up as raised risk of hormone-linked mood problems. Dorani et al. (2021), the first study of its kind, found that in women with ADHD *"[t]he prevalence of PMDD and PPD were high in ADHD, compared to the general population"* — premenstrual dysphoric disorder and postpartum depression, plus elevated climacteric mood and somatic symptoms (`hypothesis:hormone-related-mood-disorders-elevated-in-adhd`). Hormonal transitions are not just when ADHD symptoms wobble; they are when mood is most at risk.

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **Sex-skewed evidence, again.** As with [[literature/adhd-in-women-and-girls|women and girls]], much of what this notebook borrows was measured without controlling for hormonal status (§1's neglect point), so results may not transfer cleanly — one more reason to read the evidence base carefully.
- **If the reader is a woman, this is directly relevant.** Symptom or mood swings that track the cycle, or that shifted around pregnancy, contraception or perimenopause, are a recognised pattern (§2–3), not random or a failure of willpower — worth charting over time and raising explicitly at an [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]], where a single visit cannot capture cyclical variation.
- **It connects mood and sleep.** Hormone-linked mood risk (§3) ties into the [[literature/adhd-and-mood|mood]] differential, and reproductive hormones interact with the circadian system behind [[literature/adhd-and-sleep|sleep]] — so hormones are a thread through several of the notebook's questions, not a separate topic.
