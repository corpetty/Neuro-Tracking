The personal record contains one positive self-administered screen and no formal evaluation ([[about-me/diagnostics|diagnostic history]]). The open `question:next-clinical-step` asks what a real evaluation involves, who does it, and what to bring. This note answers that from the guideline literature. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adult-adhd-assessment.jsonl`.

The short version: a screen is a doorway, not a diagnosis. A guideline-grade evaluation is a clinical interview that establishes the pattern across the lifespan, checks that impairment is real, rules out the mimics, and — crucially — does not rest on the person's own memory alone.

## 1. A screen is not a diagnosis

This is the firmest conclusion in the field. The World Federation of ADHD consensus statement (Faraone et al., 2021), which distils the evidence into 208 numbered conclusions, states in Conclusion 14: *"ADHD can only be diagnosed by a licensed clinician who interviews the parent or caregiver and/or patient to document criteria for the disorder. It cannot be diagnosed by rating scales alone, neuropsychological tests, or methods for imaging the brain."* The European consensus (Kooij et al., 2019) says the same from the assessment side: *"The clinical interview is essential for diagnosing ADHD in adults, which investigates the characteristic symptoms and impairments of ADHD in both childhood and adulthood."*

That is what `hypothesis:no-single-measure-is-diagnostic` asserts, now with guideline backing. It also fixes the status of the ASRS result precisely: a positive screen is exactly a reason to seek a clinical evaluation, not a verdict (`hypothesis:screening-warrants-evaluation`). The screen did its job; the next tier is a clinician.

## 2. What the interview actually covers

A guideline-grade interview — the **DIVA-5** (`instrument:diva-5`) is the canonical structured tool, walking each DSM symptom across childhood and adulthood with real-life examples — establishes four things the screen cannot:

- **The pattern across the lifespan.** Not just current symptoms, but their presence in childhood, because ADHD is defined as neurodevelopmental with early onset.
- **Impairment, not just symptoms.** Whether the pattern actually degrades work, relationships, and daily function — the threshold that separates a trait from a disorder.
- **The differential.** Ruling in or out the mimics this notebook tracks elsewhere — anxiety, mood, sleep — since their symptoms overlap ADHD's (see [[literature/adhd-and-anxiety|ADHD & anxiety]] and [[dimensions/arousal-and-mind-wandering|Arousal, state regulation, and mind-wandering]]).
- **Comorbidity.** Kooij et al. treat establishing comorbid conditions as part of the assessment, not an afterthought.

## 3. The hardest part: proving childhood onset in an adult

DSM requires that some symptoms were present in childhood. For an adult with no contemporaneous records, that has to be reconstructed from memory — and memory is unreliable here. Mannuzza et al. (2002), in a 16-year prospective follow-up, tested exactly this and found retrospective recall wanting: *"Positive predictive value was 0.27, i.e., of all adults retrospectively given a diagnosis of childhood ADHD, only 27% would be correctly identified."* Their remedy is explicit: *"[t]he results stress the importance of obtaining contemporaneous information on childhood symptoms in establishing a childhood history of ADHD."*

The consequence is that **your own account is not enough**, and this is not a slight — it is a measurement fact (`hypothesis:childhood-onset-hard-to-verify-retrospectively`). Sibley et al. (2012) show the same in young adults: *"Parent report was more diagnostically sensitive than self-report. Young adults with ADHD tended to underreport current symptoms, while young adults without ADHD tended to overreport symptoms."* Kooij et al. reach the practical conclusion that where recall is poor, *"the presence of a collateral informant (generally a parent or spouse) is of great value."*

**But whom to believe is itself contested** (`tension:self-report-vs-informant`). Sibley's data say parents are more sensitive and adults with ADHD underreport; Kooij notes that in adults the patient may actually be the best informant, while a collateral still adds value. The discrepancy between sources is informative rather than noise — and it feeds the live `tension:late-onset-vs-late-identification`, since a first screen in adulthood cannot by itself say whether the pattern was always there and missed, or is something else.

## 4. What to bring, and to whom

Turning this into a concrete next step:

- **Who.** A clinician licensed to diagnose — typically a psychiatrist or a clinical psychologist experienced with adult ADHD; a GP can refer. A self-report screen, however positive, cannot substitute.
- **A developmental history.** Anything contemporaneous from childhood: school reports, report-card comments about attention or restlessness, old assessments. Mannuzza's finding is the reason these matter more than memory.
- **A collateral informant.** A parent who remembers childhood, and/or a partner who sees current daily function — Kooij's "collateral informant" and Sibley's "parent report."
- **The differential, explicit.** The internal-state material from [[literature/internal-restlessness-and-cognitive-disengagement|Internal restlessness & cognitive disengagement]] and the anxiety/sleep/mood rivals, so the evaluator can trace the symptom to its source rather than assume the ADHD thread.
- **Impairment examples.** Concrete instances where the pattern cost something, since impairment is the threshold, not symptom count.

This is the one-page summary `question:next-clinical-step` calls for: bring the screen as the reason for the visit, the developmental and collateral information the diagnosis actually rests on, and the differential you want traced.
