Most of this notebook is about mechanisms and differentials. This note is about the human cost, because it is often the part that actually brings people to a first evaluation: the sense of being lazy, unreliable, "not living up to potential" — a low opinion of oneself accumulated over years. The research reframes that self-story as a *consequence* of ADHD rather than a truth about character, and, importantly, as something that can change. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-self-esteem.jsonl`.

## 1. Lower self-esteem, across the lifespan

The finding is consistent from childhood to adulthood. In children and adolescents, Mazzone et al. (2013) found that *"ADHD subjects revealed lower scores on all self-esteem domains compared to controls,"* with far more of them in the pathological range. In adults, Cook et al.'s (2014) systematic review concluded that *"there is evidence to suggest that ADHD is associated with lower self-esteem in adulthood and that self-esteem difficulties can be remedied, to at least some extent, by psychotherapeutic work"* (`hypothesis:adhd-lowers-self-esteem`). And over the long term, Harpin et al. (2016), reviewing 127 studies, found that *"[u]ntreated ADHD was associated with poorer long-term self-esteem and social function outcomes compared with non-ADHD controls."*

## 2. Built, not intrinsic

The mechanism is accumulation, not a direct symptom (`mechanism:low-self-esteem`). Cook et al. (2014) describe how *"[i]ndividuals with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) often grow up with negative messages surrounding their abilities and may experience adverse outcomes throughout their lives."* Years of missed deadlines, forgotten commitments, being told to try harder, and falling short of one's own evident ability lay down a negative self-concept. This matters for the wider picture: low self-esteem is a plausible link in the demoralisation pathway by which ADHD raises the risk of depression ([[literature/adhd-and-mood|ADHD & mood]], `hypothesis:adhd-raises-depression-risk`), and it overlaps the self-blame reframed in [[literature/adhd-and-motivation|ADHD & motivation]].

## 3. It is not fixed

The hopeful and well-supported half: self-esteem is modifiable. Harpin et al. (2016) found that *"[t]reatment for ADHD was associated with improvement in outcomes; however, further long-term outcome studies are needed,"* with improvement in the large majority of self-esteem outcomes, and Cook et al. note the same remediability through psychotherapeutic work (`hypothesis:self-esteem-improves-with-treatment`). A negative self-concept accumulated over years is not a permanent verdict; it responds to recognition, treatment and support.

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **The self-story may be a symptom's shadow, not the truth.** If years of "I should be doing better" have hardened into a low sense of worth, §1–2 say that is a recognised consequence of an unrecognised pattern, not a character fact — which is much of why organising this notebook is itself useful.
- **It connects the threads.** Low self-esteem sits between the practical difficulties and the mood risk ([[literature/adhd-and-mood|mood]]); tracking it is part of taking the [[literature/adhd-comorbidity-overview|whole picture]] seriously, not a separate issue.
- **Recognition is already part of the remedy.** §3 says the self-concept can improve with treatment and support, and simply reframing accumulated failures as an explicable pattern — rather than a personal failing — is a first step, whatever the [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]] concludes.
