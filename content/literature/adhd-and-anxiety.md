The load-bearing personal tension in this notebook is that *the same internal restlessness fits ADHD and anxiety equally well* (`tension:adhd-vs-anxiety-internal-restlessness`). This note takes that tension seriously with the literature, in three moves: how often the two actually co-occur, why their symptoms overlap so badly, and what — if anything — reliably separates them. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-anxiety.jsonl`.

The conclusion up front: the overlap is real and large, so it cannot be reasoned away; what discriminates the two is not how intense the symptoms feel but their *structure* — onset, trajectory, and whether they persist when the worry is absent.

## 1. The overlap is real, not a measurement artefact

If ADHD and anxiety only *looked* similar on a questionnaire, the fix would be a better questionnaire. But they genuinely travel together. In the nationally representative US National Comorbidity Survey Replication, Kessler et al. (2006) found current adult ADHD at 4.4% prevalence and reported that *"[a]dult ADHD was highly comorbid with many other DSM-IV disorders assessed in the survey and was associated with substantial role impairment."* Katzman et al. (2017) put an upper bound on it: *"As many as 80% of adults with ADHD have at least one coexisting psychiatric disorder,"* with mood and anxiety disorders among the most frequent. Focusing on anxiety specifically, Jarrett & Ollendick (2008) note that *"approximately 25% of children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) exhibit an anxiety disorder"* (`hypothesis:adhd-anxiety-frequently-comorbid`).

That is the honest starting point. A person with ADHD is *more* likely than average to also have anxiety, so "you're just anxious" and "you have ADHD" are not mutually exclusive, and the base rates alone will not settle an individual case.

## 2. Why the symptoms overlap: shared surface, and emotional dysregulation

Two things drive the overlap. The first is plain symptom sharing: Katzman et al. (2017) observe that *"[o]verlapping symptoms between ADHD and comorbid psychopathologies represent challenges for diagnosis and treatment."* Restlessness, poor concentration, and a racing mind are on both maps.

The second is more specific and more interesting: **emotional dysregulation** (`mechanism:emotional-dysregulation`). Shaw et al. (2014) conclude that *"emotion dysregulation is prevalent in ADHD throughout the lifespan and is a major contributor to impairment,"* and propose that it *"may arise from deficits in orienting toward, recognizing, and/or allocating attention to emotional stimuli; these deficits implicate dysfunction within a striato-amygdalo-medial prefrontal cortical network"* — i.e. it is tied back to the attentional core rather than free-floating. Katzman et al. (2017) agree it is characteristic: *"[s]tudies have concluded that emotional dysregulation is a distinctive attribute of adult ADHD psychopathology."*

**But its status is genuinely unsettled**, and this is worth reifying rather than glossing (`tension:emotional-dysregulation-core-vs-distinct`). Shaw et al. lay out three competing models with different predictions: *"emotion dysregulation and ADHD are correlated but distinct dimensions; emotion dysregulation is a core diagnostic feature of ADHD; and the combination constitutes a nosological entity distinct from both ADHD and emotion dysregulation alone."* So `hypothesis:emotional-dysregulation-is-core-adhd` is one reading of three, not a settled fact — which is exactly why the same paper both supports and pressure-tests it in the graph. For the personal case, the practical point is that a churning, emotionally-charged internal state could be ADHD's own emotional dysregulation *or* a comorbid anxiety disorder, and the two share a substrate.

## 3. Which way does it run? Directionality is open

Because the two co-occur so often, a natural question is which causes which — and the literature does not answer it (`tension:adhd-anxiety-directionality`). Three orderings compete, and each has a different clinical implication:

1. **ADHD → anxiety.** Years of ADHD-driven underperformance and unpredictability breed a secondary anxiety. Treating the ADHD should then relieve the anxiety.
2. **Anxiety → apparent ADHD.** Worry and hypervigilance fragment attention and produce restlessness that *looks* like ADHD. Treating the anxiety should then relieve the "ADHD."
3. **Shared cause.** Common temperament or genetics generate both, and neither is downstream of the other.

Jarrett & Ollendick (2008) treat the temporal relations between ADHD and anxiety as one of the substantive open questions the field must resolve, alongside temperament and genetics. Nothing in the personal record currently distinguishes these orderings, so the differential is held open rather than collapsed into one label — which is also the stance of `question:adhd-anxiety-sleep-or-mood`.

## 4. What actually discriminates them: structure, not intensity

If intensity cannot separate ADHD from anxiety — a severe version of each feels equally disruptive — then the discriminating information has to be structural. Jarrett & Ollendick (2008) point exactly there, arguing that progress *"will be advanced through a more precise phenotypic classification of ADHD and the integration of research in adjacent fields such as temperament and genetics."* That is the research-level version of a clinical rule of thumb, and it lines up with `hypothesis:structure-not-intensity-discriminates`:

- **Onset.** ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition with childhood roots; an anxiety disorder can begin at any age. When did the pattern actually start?
- **Trajectory.** ADHD symptoms are relatively trait-like and situation-general; anxiety symptoms tend to track stressors and wax and wane with them.
- **Counterfactual.** The sharpest question: *is the inattention and restlessness present even when there is nothing to be anxious about?* Attention problems that persist in calm, low-stakes conditions point toward ADHD; ones that appear only under worry point toward anxiety.

None of these is a lab test, and Katzman et al.'s (2017) pragmatic guidance is that when the conditions genuinely co-occur, the most impairing or destabilizing one is treated first and the comorbidities addressed in a stepwise fashion — the differential does not have to be fully resolved before anything can be done.

## Where this leaves the personal case

This note does not break the central tension; it sharpens what would break it. The overlap is real (§1) and even shares a mechanism (§2), so no amount of introspecting on *how bad* the internal state feels will separate the two. The information that would is structural (§4): the age of onset, whether the pattern tracks stressors, and above all whether it is present when calm. Those are the things worth pinning down before an evaluation — they connect directly to the still-open `question:what-is-the-internal-state` and feed the concrete summary a clinician needs (`question:next-clinical-step`).
