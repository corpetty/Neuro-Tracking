This notebook works through the ADHD differential one condition at a time — [[literature/adhd-and-anxiety|anxiety]], [[literature/adhd-and-mood|mood]], [[literature/adhd-and-sleep|sleep]], [[literature/autism-and-adhd|autism]]. This note steps back to the pattern across them, because the single most important fact about ADHD comorbidity reframes the whole exercise: co-occurrence is the norm, so the question is rarely "ADHD *or* X" and usually "which of these co-occur, and how do they interact." Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-comorbidity-overview.jsonl`.

## 1. Comorbidity is the rule, not the exception

Most people with ADHD do not have ADHD alone. Katzman et al. (2017) state it plainly: *"[a]s many as 80% of adults with ADHD have at least one coexisting psychiatric disorder,"* most often mood and anxiety disorders. The nationally representative data agree — Kessler et al. (2006) found that *"[a]dult ADHD was highly comorbid with many other DSM-IV disorders assessed in the survey and was associated with substantial role impairment"* (`hypothesis:comorbidity-is-the-rule-in-adhd`). The recurring conditions are anxiety, depression, substance use, personality and learning disorders, autism, and the sleep problems covered elsewhere here.

## 2. Substance use: the comorbidity this notebook had not yet covered

One major comorbidity deserves its own mention because it runs strongly in both directions. Prospectively, Lee et al. (2011) meta-analysed longitudinal cohorts and found *"[c]hildren with ADHD were also more likely to develop disorders of abuse/dependence for nicotine, alcohol, marijuana, cocaine, and other substances (i.e., unspecified)"* — ADHD is an early risk factor for later substance problems. And the reverse: van Emmerik-van Oortmerssen et al. (2012) found that *"[o]verall, 23.1% (CI: 19.4-27.2%) of all SUD subjects met DSM-criteria for comorbid ADHD"* (`hypothesis:adhd-elevates-substance-use-risk`). Roughly one in four people in addiction treatment has ADHD, and childhood ADHD raises the odds of getting there.

## 3. Why "and" matters more than "or"

The clinical consequence is that framing every symptom as ADHD *versus* a rival is usually the wrong shape. Overlapping symptoms mean the conditions genuinely co-occur, not merely resemble each other, which is why the per-condition notes keep landing on entanglement rather than exclusion. It also drives sequencing: Katzman et al.'s guidance is to treat the most impairing or destabilising condition first and address the others in a stepwise fashion. So a positive ADHD screen does not have to be defended *against* anxiety, mood or sleep — all of them can be present, and part of the work is mapping which.

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **The differential is a map, not a single choice.** The individual notes on [[literature/adhd-and-anxiety|anxiety]], [[literature/adhd-and-mood|mood]] and [[literature/adhd-and-sleep|sleep]] are not competitors to be narrowed to one winner; §1 says several may hold at once, so the goal of an [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]] is to chart the whole picture.
- **Substance use is worth surfacing honestly.** Given the two-way link (§2), any pattern of substance use — including alcohol or nicotine as coping — is relevant context for an evaluation, in both directions.
- **Comorbidity does not weaken the ADHD question — it complicates it.** Finding anxiety or low mood alongside does not explain ADHD away (that was the point of each differential note); it means the assessment has to hold more than one thread and decide what to address first.
