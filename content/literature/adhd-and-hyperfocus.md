"But I can concentrate for hours on things I care about" is one of the most common reasons people talk themselves out of ADHD. Hyperfocus is the name for that experience, and it looks like a contradiction: an attention *deficit* that can also lock onto a task and refuse to let go. This note takes the paradox seriously — what hyperfocus is, what reframe it forces, and how thin the evidence still is. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-hyperfocus.jsonl`.

## 1. The paradox, stated plainly

Hupfeld et al. (2019), introducing the Adult Hyperfocus Questionnaire, put the puzzle directly: *"Adults with ADHD often report episodes of long-lasting, highly focused attention, a surprising report given their tendency to be distracted by irrelevant information. This has been colloquially termed 'hyperfocus' (HF)"* (`mechanism:hyperfocus`). Ashinoff & Abu-Akel (2021) give the phenomenon a careful definition: *"'Hyperfocus' is a phenomenon that reflects one's complete absorption in a task, to a point where a person appears to completely ignore or 'tune out' everything else."*

## 2. The reframe: dysregulation, not deficit

The resolution is that ADHD may not be a *shortage* of attention at all but a failure to *regulate* where it goes. Ozel-Kizil et al. (2016) frame it exactly this way: *"Patients with Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) suffer not only from inability to focus but also from inability to shift attention for events that trigger their interests."* Both the drifting and the locking-on are the same underlying problem — attention deployed in the wrong amounts at the wrong times (`hypothesis:adhd-is-attention-dysregulation-not-deficit`). Hyperfocus is the *over*-deployed pole; the distractibility is the *under*-deployed one. On this view, being able to disappear into an absorbing task is not evidence *against* ADHD; it is the other face of the same dysregulation, and it connects to the executive-control account in [[literature/adhd-and-executive-function|ADHD & executive function]].

## 3. The evidence: real signal, young and non-specific

Hyperfocus does track ADHD empirically. Hupfeld et al. (2019) found across a pilot and a replication sample that *"[t]hose with higher ADHD symptomology reported higher total and dispositional HF and more frequent HF across each of the three settings (school, hobbies, and screen time) as well as on a fourth subscale describing real-world HF scenarios."* Ozel-Kizil et al. (2016) found the same in a clinical comparison: *"Hyperfocusing is higher in adult ADHD and there was no difference between stimulant-naive patients or patients on stimulants. Hyperfocusing can be defined as a separate dimension of adult ADHD"* (`hypothesis:hyperfocus-elevated-in-adhd`).

But the honest caveats are large. Ashinoff & Abu-Akel (2021) — the critical review — note that *"[h]yperfocus is most often mentioned in the context of autism, schizophrenia, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, but research into its effect on cognitive and neural functioning is limited,"* and that the concept is poorly and inconsistently defined across studies. So the finding rests on new self-report questionnaires, modest samples, and a construct that is neither settled nor specific to ADHD. Elevated in ADHD, yes; a hallmark of it, not established.

## Where this leaves the personal case

- **It disarms a common self-rebuttal.** "I can hyperfocus, so I can't have ADHD" does not follow. §2 says intense absorption is consistent with — not contrary to — an attention-*regulation* problem, so it should not be used to close the question.
- **It is not diagnostic.** Hyperfocus occurs in autism, schizophrenia and the general population (§3), so its presence neither confirms ADHD nor distinguishes it from the [[literature/autism-and-adhd|autism overlap]] or anything else — it describes, like the other transdiagnostic features in this notebook.
- **Worth reporting as experience.** If bouts of lose-track-of-time absorption (and difficulty being pulled out of them) are part of the pattern, they belong in an [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|evaluation]] as lived experience — one more piece of the attention-regulation picture, not a test result and not a verdict.
