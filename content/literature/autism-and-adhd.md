Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is the other neurodevelopmental condition that shadows ADHD, and any honest differential has to account for it. This note lays out what is known about the overlap and, as with the [[literature/adhd-and-anxiety|anxiety differential]], keeps the boundary question explicit. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/autism-and-adhd.jsonl`.

> [!note] Scope
> Nothing in the personal record reports autistic traits ([[about-me/symptoms|symptom log]]). This note is here for differential completeness and because the ADHD literature cannot be read cleanly without it — not because ASD is currently on the table for this case.

## 1. They co-occur far above chance

The two conditions travel together often. Rommelse et al. (2010) summarise the epidemiology: *"Evidence indicates both disorders co-occur with a high frequency, in 20-50% of children with ADHD meeting criteria for ASD and in 30-80% of ASD children meeting criteria for ADHD."* Those are large fractions in both directions (`hypothesis:adhd-autism-frequently-co-occur`).

The clinical literature was slow to study this, for a bureaucratic reason worth knowing. Leitner (2014) notes that *"[t]he DSM-IV had specified that an ASD diagnosis is an exclusion criterion for ADHD, thereby limiting research of this common clinical co-occurrence."* DSM-5 removed that bar and now permits a dual diagnosis, which is why the overlap is only recently being mapped properly.

## 2. The overlap reaches down to shared causes

This is not just two symptom lists that happen to resemble each other. Rommelse et al. (2010) conclude that *"family and twin studies do provide support for the hypothesis that ADHD and ASD originate from partly similar familial/genetic factors,"* with some candidate pleiotropic genes. Antshel & Russo (2019) put the neurocognitive layer beside the genetic one: *"ASD and ADHD have shared genetic heritability and are both associated with shared impairments in social functioning and executive functioning"* (`hypothesis:adhd-autism-share-etiology`). So the overlap is partly a shared substrate, not only a shared surface.

## 3. Yet they remain distinct categories

The shared substrate does not collapse the clinical distinction. Antshel & Russo (2019) are explicit: *"[t]he research on ASD and ADHD suggests some overlap between the two disorders yet enough differences to indicate that these conditions are sufficiently distinct to warrant separate diagnostic categories."* Leitner (2014) says the same from the criteria side: *"both ASD and ADHD share some phenotypic similarities, but are characterized by distinct diagnostic criteria"* (`hypothesis:adhd-autism-remain-distinct-categories`).

## 4. The boundary is where it gets interesting

Sections 2 and 3 pull in opposite directions, and that is the point (`tension:adhd-autism-boundary`). The genetic and neurocognitive evidence points toward a *partly shared underlying dimension*; the phenotypic and diagnostic evidence keeps ADHD and ASD as *separate categories*. Both are well-supported, and the field has not resolved the tension — it is the same category-versus-continuum question that runs through the [[literature/dimensional-continuum-adhd|dimensional models]] survey, now at the boundary between two disorders rather than within one. It also connects to the subthreshold framing: Ogundele & Morton (2025) argue that neurodevelopmental profiles below the diagnostic line still carry real impairment, which blurs any hard edge between "has ADHD," "has autism," and "has neither."

## Where this leaves the personal case

For this case the takeaway is modest but real. There are no reported autistic traits, so ASD is not an active hypothesis here. But two things follow from the overlap. First, the shared executive- and attention-related impairments mean that some of what reads as ADHD-flavoured inattention is not unique to ADHD, which is one more reason a screen is not a diagnosis ([[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|the adult assessment]]). Second, a thorough evaluation should at least register whether any social-communication pattern is present, precisely because DSM-5 now allows both to be recognised where both are there. This rounds out the differential alongside anxiety, sleep, mood, and cognitive disengagement rather than adding a new front-runner.
