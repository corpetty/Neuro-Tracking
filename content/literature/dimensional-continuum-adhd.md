A working survey of where the "is ADHD a category or a continuum?" question stands. The short version: across four largely independent lines of evidence — the *latent structure* of symptom data, *quantitative genetics*, *measurement*, and *transdiagnostic classification* — the weight of findings favours a dimensional model, in which clinical ADHD is the extreme end of a trait that is continuously distributed in the general population. That is not a settled consensus, and this note keeps the counter-evidence visible. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source; each is also carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/dimensional-continuum-adhd.jsonl`.

This is literature, not diagnosis. It exists here to give the personal [[about-me/connections|dimensional-placement]] framing a defensible foundation — and to make clear exactly how strong (and how contested) that foundation is.

## 1. Latent structure: does the data contain a category boundary?

Taxometric methods (Meehl's MAXCOV / MAMBAC family) ask whether a set of indicators is generated by a discrete latent class (a "taxon") or by a continuous latent dimension. Applied to ADHD, they have repeatedly failed to find a taxon.

The canonical review of this literature, Coghill & Sonuga-Barke (2012), summarises three taxometric studies of ADHD and concludes: *"Taken together, these analyses suggested that ADHD is best modelled as a continuum in both children and adolescents, thus supporting the genetic and other evidence that suggests a dimensional structure and multifactorial aetiology."* The largest of those (Haslam et al., 2006; ~3,000 children) *"found no evidence for a discrete taxon,"* and the NICHD-sample analysis of Marcus & Barry (2011) *"support[ed] a dimensional rather than categorical latent structure for ADHD as well as for the separate inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity domains."*

McLennan (2016) makes the point in the sharpest terms a clinician-facing journal will allow: *"Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder does not appear to have a taxon."* He also notes that neuropsychological performance varies *linearly* with symptom load "with no evidence of discontinuity" — there is no natural break where "normal" ends and "disorder" begins.

**The honest counter-point.** The dimensional read is not unanimous. Fassbender (2018), reviewing translational work, reports that some analyses recover categorical structure *within* clinical samples: *"Taxometric analysis outcomes were more consistent with a categorical structure to the data in the group with ADHD and a dimensional structure in the typically developing group."* This is the live tension the field has not closed — see §5 below and `tension:dimensional-vs-categorical-adhd`.

## 2. Quantitative genetics: clinical ADHD as the tail of a heritable trait

Twin and molecular-genetic work is the strongest pillar of the continuum view, because it tests whether the *causes* are the same across the trait range, not just whether the scores look smooth.

Faraone & Larsson's (2019) review of the twin literature concludes: *"Both studies suggest that the diagnosis of ADHD is the extreme of a continuous distribution of ADHD symptoms in the population and that the etiologic factors involved in the disorder also account for the full range of symptoms."* The heritability is essentially the same at the diagnostic extreme and across sub-threshold variation — *"a strong genetic link between the extreme and the sub-threshold variation of DSM-IV ADHD symptoms"* (the Larsson et al., 2012 study of 16,366 twins). This is the key move: if the diagnosis were a distinct condition, you would expect distinct genetics at the tail. You don't find them.

Molecular genetics now says the same thing from the common-variant side. The first well-powered ADHD GWAS (Demontis et al., 2019; >20,000 cases) states in its abstract: *"Strong concordance with GWAS of quantitative population measures of ADHD symptoms supports that clinical diagnosis of ADHD is an extreme expression of continuous heritable traits."* Polygenic scores built from clinical case-control data predict continuous attention ratings in unselected population children, and scores built from population traits predict clinical diagnosis — the liability runs in both directions across the same axis.

## 3. Measurement: instruments built for a continuum

If ADHD is a trait, symptom checklists should be able to score the whole distribution, not just flag cases. The instruments have moved in exactly that direction.

The ASRS v1.1 used in the personal record ([[about-me/diagnostics|diagnostic history]]) was designed by Kessler et al. (2005) as a symptom-*frequency* scale whose *"[o]ptimal scoring to predict clinical syndrome classifications was to sum unweighted dichotomous responses across all 18 ASRS questions"* — a dimensional score, with the six-item Part A retained only as a fast screen. The Conners adult scales (CAARS) are scored as T-scores against population norms, i.e. as standardised positions on a distribution rather than a yes/no.

The clearest statement of intent is the SWAN scale, which deliberately rates each DSM symptom on a symmetric −3 (strength) to +3 (weakness) scale so that *both* tails are measured; validation work (Burton et al., 2019) frames the whole enterprise this way: *"ADHD is now widely considered to be the extreme end of widely distributed, quantitative traits … Although diagnostic categories are useful in clinical practice, dichotomizing continuously distributed traits into case/control reduces statistical power in research."* Unlike conventional checklists — which pile up at zero in the general population — SWAN scores come out roughly normally distributed, which is the empirical signature of an underlying continuum. On internal structure, current models favour a bifactor solution: one dominant general ADHD factor with weak specific inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity factors (Rodenacker et al., 2017).

## 4. Transdiagnostic frameworks: ADHD inside continuous spectra

The major post-DSM classification programmes are all dimensional, and each places ADHD on a spectrum rather than in a box.

**RDoC** (Insel et al., 2010) reorganises psychopathology around functional dimensions measured across their full range: investigators are *"encouraged to explicate the full range of a given dimension to develop thresholds for different types of interventions."* Its constructs include the executive- and impulse-control functions central to ADHD, studied as brain-circuit dimensions that cut across diagnoses.

**HiTOP** (Kotov et al., 2017) locates ADHD empirically within the externalizing super-spectrum: *"The traditional externalizing dimension captures comorbidity among substance use disorders, oppositional defiant disorder (ODD), conduct disorder, adult antisocial behavior, intermittent explosive disorder (IED), and attention-deficit-hyperactivity disorder (ADHD),"* with disinhibition as its principal trait substrate.

**The p-factor** (Caspi et al., 2014) found that *"[p]sychiatric disorders were initially explained by three higher-order factors … but explained even better with one General Psychopathology dimension,"* on which ADHD loads via the externalizing pole — pushing the categorical picture even further toward a graded, general-liability model.

## 5. Implications for diagnosis and thresholds

If the trait is continuous, the DSM-5 symptom count (6/9 for children, lowered to 5/9 for adults) is a cut on a slope rather than a join between two kinds of people — and the evidence increasingly says the slope has no kink. Arildskov et al. (2024) tracked psychosocial quality of life against ADHD-trait level and found *"[p]sychosocial QoL gradually declines in a linear manner as ADHD trait levels increase providing further support for a dimensional model,"* with no breakpoint at the high end. McLennan (2016) draws the practical consequence: *"the lack of discontinuity in the distribution of such traits in the population creates great uncertainty as to what thresholds should prompt explicit intervention."* Consistent with this, sub-threshold ADHD carries real impairment across a wide range of functional domains relative to unaffected peers (Morton, 2025), and DSM-5 has itself edged toward dimensional thinking by relaxing the adult threshold.

**The counter-argument, kept live.** A continuum does not license lowering the bar indefinitely. Kazda et al.'s (2021) scoping review found that *"ADHD is overdiagnosed in children and adolescents[; f]or individuals with milder symptoms in particular, the harms associated with an ADHD diagnosis may often outweigh the benefits."* A dimension still needs a *clinically justified* action threshold — impairment, not trait level alone, is what should trigger intervention. That, plus Fassbender's categorical-within-cases result (§1), is why the dimensional-vs-categorical question is held open here rather than declared won.

## Where this leaves the personal case

The literature supports treating an ASRS result as a *location on a trait*, not a verdict — which is what `hypothesis:dimensional-placement` claims. But two caveats travel with it: the continuum is real yet its *action* threshold is a clinical judgement (Kazda), and locating oneself on it still depends on ruling out the mimics that produce the same scores (anxiety, sleep, mood — see [[about-me/connections|literature connections]] and `tension:adhd-vs-anxiety-internal-restlessness`). The open task is [[about-me/diagnostics|computing the summed ASRS score]] so the placement can actually be made (`question:where-on-continuum`).
