Sleep already appears in this notebook as a *rival*: sleep loss degrades attention, so it has to be excluded before an ADHD reading of inattention is trusted. That case is made in [[dimensions/arousal-and-mind-wandering|Arousal, state regulation, and mind-wandering]] and carried by `hypothesis:sleep-rivals-inattention-not-restlessness`. This note develops the directions that framing leaves out — ADHD *disrupts* sleep, and a delayed body clock may be part of ADHD rather than a look-alike — and reifies the resulting three-way question. Every quotation below is verbatim from the cited source and is carried as an evidence triple in `interpretive/adhd-and-sleep.jsonl`.

## 1. The relationship runs both ways

The mimic direction (sleep loss → inattention) is only one arrow. Hvolby's (2015) review is explicit that *"[t]he relationships of ADHD with sleep problems, psychiatric comorbidities and medications are complex and multidirectional."* Bijlenga et al. (2019) lay the arrows out individually: *"ADHD is either the cause or the consequence of sleep disturbances, or they may have a shared etiological and genetic background."* So `hypothesis:sleep-adhd-bidirectional` is the honest starting point — treating sleep only as a rival to rule out understates a genuinely two-way (or three-way) link.

## 2. A delayed body clock, highly prevalent and maybe intrinsic

The most striking strand is circadian. Beyond quantity of sleep, people with ADHD tend to run *late* — a phase-delayed clock (`mechanism:circadian-delay`). Van Veen et al. (2010) measured it objectively with actigraphy and salivary melatonin and found *"diurnal rhythm deviations during everyday life in the majority of adults with ADHD that have SOI"* (sleep-onset insomnia), including a delayed melatonin onset relative to controls. Bijlenga et al. (2019) put a number on how common it is: ADHD *"is highly associated with the delayed sleep phase disorder, a circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorder, which is prevalent in 73-78% of children and adults with ADHD."*

At those rates it is hard to file the delay as incidental comorbidity, which is why the same authors go further and *"propose a novel view on ADHD, where a part of the ADHD symptoms are the result of chronic sleep disorders, with most evidence for the delayed circadian rhythm as the underlying mechanism"* (`hypothesis:adhd-circadian-delay-intrinsic`). That is a proposal, not a settled fact — hence its lower confidence in the graph — but it moves sleep, for a subgroup, from *mimic* toward *component*.

## 3. Three readings of the same observation

Sections 1 and 2 leave a genuinely open question (`tension:sleep-rival-or-consequence`). Given someone with poor sleep and inattention, the disturbed sleep could be:

1. **A mimic** — the inattention is sleep-driven, not ADHD (the rival view, `hypothesis:sleep-rivals-inattention-not-restlessness`).
2. **A consequence** — ADHD's disorganisation and late clock degrade sleep, which then worsens daytime symptoms.
3. **A shared mechanism** — a common circadian or genetic factor produces both.

Bijlenga's "cause or consequence or shared" names all three, and the evidence supports each in different samples. The record cannot say which applies to a given person, so this note holds the sleep thread open rather than filing it as "just a mimic."

## Where this leaves the personal case

The practical consequences are concrete and mostly agree with the [[literature/adult-adhd-assessment|assessment note]], which already flags that guidelines recommend evaluating sleep before attributing symptoms or starting medication. For this case:

- **Sleep has to be characterised, not assumed.** Bedtime, sleep-onset latency, and especially *timing* — is the clock simply late? — matter, because a phase delay is a different finding from short sleep.
- **The direction is itself informative.** If inattention tracks nights of poor sleep, that favours the mimic reading; if the clock is chronically delayed and symptoms persist even after adequate sleep, that points toward ADHD with a circadian component.
- **It does not collapse the differential.** As with [[literature/adhd-and-anxiety|anxiety]], sleep both overlaps ADHD and can be part of it, so no single night's data settles the question — the structural evidence still carries the diagnosis.
