## Where this started

I took the **ASRS v1.1** — the Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale, a screening tool developed with the WHO. It's a *screening* instrument, not a diagnostic one: its job is to flag "worth looking into further," not to hand out a diagnosis.

**My results:**

- **Part A: 4 / 6** — the six-question screener that actually determines the result. The threshold for a positive screen is 4 or more, so I landed right at the cutoff. Part A is considered the most predictive part of the instrument. My result counts as a **positive screen** — consistent with adult ADHD, and further evaluation is reasonable.
- **Part B: 6** — the twelve supplementary questions. Part B has no clean pass/fail line; it fills in *which* symptoms show up and how strongly. My 6 indicates a meaningful number of additional symptoms, backing up the Part A result.

**The honest caveat:** a positive screen is not a diagnosis. The ASRS is deliberately tuned to catch people who *might* have ADHD, so some who screen positive turn out not to once properly assessed. Plenty of things overlap — anxiety, sleep issues, thyroid problems, depression. A real diagnosis needs a person (psychiatrist, psychologist, or a GP who can refer).

## ADHD isn't one single picture

My therapist — who is clinically diagnosed with ADHD — pointed out that ADHD presents differently in different people.

The DSM-5 no longer calls these separate "types" — it calls them **presentations**. It's one condition showing up in different patterns, and a person's presentation can shift over their lifetime.

Symptoms fall into two groups:

- **Inattention** — trouble sustaining focus, easily distracted, losing things, forgetfulness, difficulty following through, avoiding tasks needing sustained mental effort, getting overwhelmed by organization.
- **Hyperactivity-impulsivity** — restlessness, fidgeting, feeling driven "as if by a motor," talking excessively, interrupting, difficulty waiting, acting before thinking.

From those come **three presentations:**

1. **Predominantly Inattentive** — inattention dominates, little hyperactivity. Historically missed because it doesn't match the stereotype; a big reason many people, especially women and girls, went undiagnosed until adulthood.
2. **Predominantly Hyperactive-Impulsive** — the reverse; least common, usually spotted earliest because it's most visible.
3. **Combined** — meets the threshold for both; the most common presentation.

Things that stuck with me: presentations aren't rigid (a hyperactive-impulsive kid can settle into a more inattentive pattern with age); how it *feels* inside can differ from how it *looks* outside; and "ADD" is a retired term that usually just means the inattentive presentation.

## Calm outside, disruptive inside

The most personal part. **I'm not really fidgety — but my internal state is very disruptive.**

Calm on the outside, churning on the inside is a real, well-recognized pattern, and the one most often missed. The DSM's hyperactivity items are worded around *observable behavior*, but many adults — especially those leaning inattentive — experience the restlessness **internally**: a racing mind, jumping thoughts, an inability to feel settled. The "driven as if by a motor" item, for a lot of people, is an entirely mental motor.

**Honesty note:** a disruptive internal state isn't unique to ADHD — it also fits anxiety well, and the two overlap and co-occur. Depression, chronic stress, and sleep problems can produce similar internal churn. This doesn't argue against the ADHD thread, but it's a symptom a good evaluator will want to trace to its source rather than assume.

## Open question I'm sitting with

When I say the internal state is disruptive — what does it actually look like for me? Racing/jumping thoughts? A physical-but-invisible sense of being wound up? Difficulty ever feeling relaxed? Something else? These feel similar from the inside but point in different directions. Worth journaling on.

## Next steps

- A positive ASRS screen is a reasonable reason to seek a full evaluation; bringing these results to a doctor gives a useful starting point.
- A proper evaluation will look at the whole picture, including overlapping possibilities (anxiety, sleep, mood).
- Keep tracking the internal-restlessness question above — concrete examples will help any future clinical conversation.
